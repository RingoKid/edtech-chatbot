A. STANDARDS

K.P.AC.2.c Make decisions about sentence structure and syntax in order to accommodate and influence the audience and achieve a specific purpose

K.L.GC.2.a Recognize that conventional sentences always include a subject and a predicate

K.L.GC.2.c Use simple sentences to express complete thoughts in written texts

B. OBJECTIVES

Determine the difference between action verb and be-verb sentences

Construct sentences using be-verbs to tell about the temperature

Create a picture depicting the temperature

Describe the temperature using a be-verb sentence and adjective

C. MATERIALS

Picture of sunny weather when the temperature is hot and one when it is cold

Noun pictures of wind, sun, rain, snow, fog, storm, and ice

Picture of a thermometer

Thermometer and temperature picture cut-outs for children

Composing paper

Whiteboards (optional)

D. SCRIPTED LANGUAGE CONSTRUCTION LESSON

2. Model, Explain, Engage (10 minutes)

[Explanation: The purpose of this section is to introduce and reinforce a targeted sentence structure through modeling and providing clear examples, while connecting to genre purpose and incorporating children’s vocabulary associated with what they are reading and writing about in their unit of study]

#Teacher Script:

Let’s remember that when we are writing, we have to talk about a main something. It can be a person, place, thing, or animal. What’s that called, everyone? (Children: the subject)

The subject. So, we first have to name what we are talking about—the subject, the namer. But then we have to tell the audience what our special something is doing, so we need an _____. (Children: action) We need an action, the action verb of the sentence. But we also said sometimes we don’t need to tell our audience what our main something is doing. Sometimes we just want to tell our audience something special about our subject, our main something. Be-verbs help us tell something about our subject—what they are. They aren’t doing anything. We are just telling something about that subject, the main thing we are talking about.

Let’s look at these pictures from our book about gardens. Here’s our garden snake. Now what can a snake do? (Children: slither) Yes, a snake slithers. My subject, my namer is what? (Children: snake) And what did my snake, my subject do? (Children: slithers) Where did he slither? (Children: in the garden) Say the whole thing. (Children: The snake slithers in the garden.) Now what if I want to just tell you something special or something important or some information about this snake? What if the snake was chasing mice through the garden? We could say that the snake is ______. (Children: hungry)

hungry—the snake is hungry.What else could we say? (Child: The snake is mean. )

He may be mean because he wants to eat. (Child: The snake is poisonous.) Poisonous—good word. Tell everyone what that means. (Child: response will vary).Right, some snakes have poison. If they bite you, the poison can go into your body, which can really hurt you. But the poison is usually used to sting other animals they want to hunt and eat—their prey. Sometimes they use their poison on other predators, the animals that are after them and want to eat them.

So, the snake is hungry; the snake is mean; the snake is poisonous.

Did our snake do anything? (Children: no)No, we are just saying something about the snake, not what it is doing. We use a special type of verb. It is not an action verb—it is a be-verb because it does not show action; it tells us something about the snake, something about its being. If we look here, we have “The snake slithers”—the action is slithers. But look at these sentences: “The snake is… The snake is… The snake is…” They all say the snake is ___ something. So what be-verb is being repeated to tell us these different things about the snake? (Children: is)Yes—is—that is a be-verb. It is the verb in the sentence that tells us something about our subject, not what our subject did.

Take out your whiteboards. First, let’s spell our be-verb “is.” Let’s stretch it out. Is—/i/—/s/ How many sounds? Children: Two; children draw two lines side by side)

Show me—how many letters should you have? (Children: Two).

Two, one for each sound. Is—what’s the first sound?(Children: /i/) Spell it. Is—what’s the second sound? (Children: /s/) Spell it. Read me the letters. (Children: i-s) What does it spell?(Children: Is)Good. Now get ready. We are going to do our Sentence Hunt Game. I am going to read a sentence from our book. You are listening for whether that sentence is a be-verb sentence or if it is an action verb sentence. If it is a be-verb sentence, you should hear “is.” The snake is poisonous. If you hear “is” and the sentence is just telling something special or important about your subject, your namer, then you will write is on your whiteboard. That shows that the sentence has the same be-verb pattern we are looking for. If the sentence is an action sentence, like “The snake slithers in the garden,” you will write an X to show that it is not a be-verb sentence. Watch me. My turn. My sentence is: The ladybug eats aphids. My subject is ladybug—that’s my namer, the thing I am talking about. I am thinking: what did the ladybug do? It eats, so I have an action. I also did not hear “is,” so I am going to write an X. This is not a be-verb sentence. Okay, ready. My turn. My sentence is: The tomato is ripe. Is this an action or a be-verb sentence? {Teacher waits for children to write is or an x on their whiteboards}. Show me. Yes, this is a be-verb sentence. The tomato is not doing anything. We are just saying something about the tomato—it is ripe. And I heard the special be-verb. What is that be-verb you wrote?(Children: is)

Is—yes.

Example sentences Up in the Garden and Down in the Dirt:

Action

5. Wrap-Up

[Explanation: The purpose of this section is to remind children about the target structure, how it’s different from previously learned structures and why they are important to the type of genre you are reading and writing in class].

#Teacher Script:

Everyone, we are working on writing informational text, we want to give information to the audience. Be-verb sentences tell us something about our subject. Action sentences tell us about what they do. We need both kinds of sentences so that we can tell the audience some facts, some things that are true about our topic. I am going to give you a sentence, you tell me if it is action –for an action verb sentence or being for a be-verb sentence. My turn. The rain poured from the clouds. (Children should chorally respond “Action-Verb”).

Very good. What action verb did I use? (Children should respond “poured”)

Excellent “poured”. That is my action-verb.

My turn. The weather is windy. Get Ready. Go. (Children should answer “Be-Verb”). Perfect! What was that special be-verb I used? (Children should respond “is”)

Is –perfect. Kiss your brains.
