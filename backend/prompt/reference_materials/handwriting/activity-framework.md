# Activity Framework

Transcription involves children's ability to hear, isolate, pronounce, discriminate between, and segment sounds. Focusing on sounds supports children's spelling development. At the same time, supporting children in writing letters within lines and using their letter knowledge to correctly sequence strokes, coordinated with their motor systems, strengthens handwriting so that children can spell more efficiently and develop writing fluency. Early transcription lessons should include several connected components. First, teachers should review previously taught sounds through retrieval practice, especially sounds children have recently learned and that can be used later in the lesson. Next, the teacher targets a new sound or sound pattern through phonemic awareness activities that help children hear, isolate, pronounce, discriminate between, and segment sounds that include the target sound. After children have practiced hearing and working with the sound, the lesson moves to mapping the sound to the letter through guided handwriting practice, where children learn the correct sequence of strokes to write the grapheme while using writing lines for support. Finally, children apply their learning through spelling activities that bring together listening for the sound and writing it. The spelling task should align with the earlier phonemic awareness activity so that children spell words containing the same target sound or sound pattern that was introduced and practiced earlier in the lesson.

## Review (5 min)

Order of Difficulty: Sound Echo, Tap the Sound, Sound Spark, Sort the Sound, Write the Sound, How Many Sounds

### [sound_echo] Sound Echo

The teacher says the sound first, and the children repeat the sound.

This echo routine helps children hear the sound clearly, hold it in their memory, and practice producing it accurately.

This activity is a good Review activity for struggling learners.

Children who struggle to isolate phonemes often have difficulty with the phonological loop, a working memory system that temporarily stores speech sounds. When this system is less efficient, the sound may not remain in memory long enough for the child to rehearse it—either silently or out loud. As a result, the memory of the phoneme fades quickly. For children who need additional support, modeling the sound first and then having students repeat it engages the phonological loop and strengthens the phonological trace, making it more likely that they can identify and work with the sound later in the lesson.

### [tap_that_sound] Tap that Sound

Children can keep sticky notes with the sound on it –/a/, /s/. Before beginning a new lesson have children stick some of their sound cards on their desk. The teacher provides the sound and children tap the correct sound card.

This tap-the-sound routine supports children in hearing, identifying, and discriminating between sounds.

This activity is a good Review activity for struggling to on-level learners, especially if they are in preK and early K.

This activity also reduces cognitive load on children's working memory. Because the teacher provides the sound, children can focus on identifying the phoneme and connecting it to its grapheme rather than trying to retrieve the sound independently. Providing the sound also supports children's attention to subtle differences between phonemes.

This activity works well as a review of previously learned sounds and is particularly supportive for children with limited working-memory capacity. Some children may be able to recognize or identify a sound but are not yet able to retrieve it independently. Retrieval is typically more demanding than identification, so hearing the sound first helps scaffold children's participation while strengthening their phoneme–grapheme connections.

### [sound_spark] Sound Spark

The teacher points to a letter-sound card (e.g., m) and children must chorally pronounce the sound.

This task is more demanding than Sound Echo and Tap the Sound because children must recognize the grapheme–phoneme connection and independently retrieve and pronounce the phoneme. This requires greater use of working memory. Recognition tasks are generally easier than recall tasks, which is why this activity is introduced after children have had sufficient exposure to the sound.

This activity is a good Review activity for all learners after sufficient exposure.

Children can move to Sound Spark once they demonstrate strong accuracy with Tap the Sound. A general guideline is that when children are about 90% successful with Tap the Sound, they are ready to move to Sound Spark. Children who are on level or more advanced may reach this stage more quickly, while struggling learners may need additional practice with recognition activities before transitioning to this retrieval task.

### [write_the_sound_review] Write the Sound

The teacher says a sound, and children write the letter that corresponds to that sound on a mini whiteboard. This can also be done with writing the sound at the beginning, middle, and end for more advanced students.

The purpose of this activity is not to check whether children are using the correct stroke sequence when writing the letter. Instead, it is a review activity that asks children to retrieve the grapheme that corresponds to a sound.

This activity is a good Review activity for all learners after a sufficient exposure.

Cognitively, this task is more demanding because children must recognize the sound and recall the correct grapheme without the visual support that the grapheme itself provides. For this reason, the activity is most effective after children have had sufficient opportunities to identify the sound through routines such as tap the sound and have received explicit instruction in how to correctly write the letter.

This review activity can be used with all learners. However, children who are on level or more advanced may reach this stage more quickly, while children who struggle may need additional practice with sound identification and letter formation before engaging in this retrieval task. As a review activity, children at advanced levels in kindergarten and on-level learners with sufficient exposure can start out with this review activity. Also, all first graders should be able to do this, but those who are struggling may need to do a tap the sound or sound spark activity first before moving to write the sound.

### [sort_the_sound] Sort the Sound

The teacher says a sound and children sort by either voiced or unvoiced, continuous or stops.

The purpose of this activity is to help children think more deeply about the sounds they have learned by comparing and categorizing them.

This activity is a good Review activity for all learners with modifications in the demand.

Comparing sounds supports children in retrieving and correctly pronouncing them because it draws attention to the similarities and differences between phonemes.

For children who need additional support, begin by having them sort sounds into two categories at a time. For children at more advanced levels, the activity can be extended to four categories, such as voiced stops, unvoiced stops, voiced continuous sounds, and unvoiced continuous sounds.

This activity works best after children have been introduced to several new sounds. At that point, comparison helps strengthen their ability to distinguish between sounds and pronounce them accurately from memory.

### [how_many_sounds] How Many Sounds

The teacher says a word. Children use a manipulative to count and show how many sounds.

The purpose of this activity is to prepare children for spelling. Specifically, it requires children to segment words into individual sounds, a key process needed for spelling.

This activity is a good Review activity for children who have had ample exposure and have mastered letter-sound correspondences.

The teacher should use only spelling patterns that children have already learned, such as CVC words or words with blends, so that the activity supports practice rather than introducing new patterns.

This type of review is most appropriate for children who have had ample exposure to and have mastered basic letter–sound correspondences. Some higher-level kindergarteners by the middle to late part of the year, as well as on-level to advanced first graders at the beginning of the year, would be ready for this type of review activity.

## Sound Target Focus (7 min)

Order of Difficulty: Sound Check, Word Sound Echo, Sound Hunt, Silly Sentence (good for initial and ending sound work), Stretch the Sounds, Chop the Sound, Sound-Switch-A-Roo

### [sound_check] Sound Check

The teacher says a sound. Children decide if the sound is the target sound or a different sound using thumbs up or thumbs down or lifting a picture that represents the target sound (e.g., picture of octopus for /o/).

This activity helps children listen carefully and compare a new sound to sounds they already know.

This activity works well for struggling learners during the Model, Explain, Connect phase when children are first learning to isolate phonemes.

It works particularly well for children who need extra support. After introducing a new sound, children need opportunities to remember that sound and compare it to previously learned sounds. For children who struggle, this routine reduces cognitive demand. Instead of giving a whole word and asking children to segment the beginning sound, the teacher provides the isolated sound. This removes the additional step of segmenting and allows children to focus specifically on identifying the phoneme.

This approach also helps strengthen memory for the new sound because children actively compare it with sounds they already know. Comparison is a higher-level skill that supports learning and memory. In this activity, however, the task remains well supported because the teacher provides the sound directly rather than requiring children to extract it from a word.

### [word_sound_echo] Word Sound Echo

Children repeat a word that begins with the target sound, then pronouncing the first sound in isolation (e.g., octopus /o), deciding if it is the target sound or not.

This activity helps children learn to segment and identify the initial sound in a word.

It works best for children who have already had some exposure to isolating beginning sounds and who can pronounce and discriminate between phonemes. For children who may be struggling, the activity can be modified by providing words that begin with only two possible sounds, such as /p/ and /b/. This reduces cognitive load because children only need to retrieve and pronounce two sounds when making their decision.

Children are also supported because many of the words will begin with the target sound that was previously introduced. This allows children to compare the target sound with previously learned sounds. In addition, children must segment the sound and repeat it, which further strengthens their phonological memory and use of the phonological loop.

### [sound_hunt] Sound Hunt

Teacher says a word, and children listen for either the beginning, middle, or ending sound to identify the target phoneme. Children indicate that they hear the target sound by lifting a card with the target grapheme or by placing the sound card in an Elkonin box or by providing a thumbs up that they hear the sound in the initial, medial, or final position.

This activity helps children learn to segment a word and identify whether a sound occurs in the beginning, middle, or ending position.

There are two ways teachers can use this activity.

1. Listening for the target sound in a specific position The teacher tells children which position to listen for and asks them to segment the word to determine whether the target sound occurs there.

Example (listening for the beginning sound): Teacher: "Our target sound is /b/. Listen to the word bat. Does bat begin with /b/?" Children: Yes.

Example (listening for the ending sound): Teacher: "Our target sound is /b/. Listen to the word cab. Does cab end with /b/?" Children: Yes.

For children who need additional support, the teacher can repeat the word and the sound within the question to reduce working memory demands. Example: "Does cat begin with /b/?"

For children at higher levels, the teacher can simply say the word and have children determine whether the target sound occurs in the specified position without repeating the sound.

2. Identifying where the target sound occurs The teacher says a word that contains the target sound and asks children to identify where they hear it.

Example: Teacher: "Listen to the word bat. Where do you hear the /b/ sound—at the beginning, middle, or end?" Children: Beginning.

For consonants, children typically identify the sound in the beginning or ending position (e.g., dip, sad). For vowels, children can identify the sound in the beginning or middle position (e.g., apple, sat), since vowels frequently occur in the middle of simple words.

This activity works well for children at all levels but can be adjusted depending on their development. Struggling learners can begin by focusing on beginning sounds, which supports early segmentation skills. With practice, teachers can move to ending sounds, and eventually to identifying whether a sound occurs in the beginning or end and the middle or ending position. Children at higher levels can handle multiple possibilities at once, such as determining whether a sound occurs in two positions or identifying both consonant and vowel sounds within the same activity.

### [silly_sentence] Silly Sentence

The teacher asks children to think of words that contain the target sound or sound pattern. Together, the class collaboratively builds a sentence using as many of those words as possible. Example (target sound /s/): Children might suggest words such as sun, sit, snake, sand, silly, soup, or sock. The class then works together to build a sentence using several of these words. Example sentences: The silly snake sits in the sun. Sam sees six silly snakes in the sand. The small snake sips soup

This activity asks children to isolate the beginning sound of words and then retrieve words from memory that begin with the same sound. This process supports the integrated retrieval of language features that children draw upon when composing, particularly when processing and producing sounds in words.

After generating words that contain the target sound, children then use those words to collaboratively build a sentence. This step encourages children to integrate phonological, lexical, and syntactic language skills, supporting both sound awareness and early sentence production.

This activity can be used with children across grade levels, though the demands of the task vary. Isolating the beginning sound of a word is generally easier than identifying ending sounds, and both are easier than segmenting or manipulating phonemes. However, the retrieval of multiple words and the construction of a sentence increases the linguistic and cognitive demands of the activity.

Children at all levels can do this activity, but they may require different levels of teacher support. For example, in prekindergarten, the activity is most effective when completed as a whole group with strong teacher guidance as children contribute ideas. In first grade, students can work more independently. For instance, children working with ending blends might create their own sentence using several words with the target pattern and then share it with a partner, who keeps track of how many words with that pattern were included.

### [stretch_the_sounds] Stretch the Sounds

Children are given two-, three-, four-, or five-sound words. Using Elkonin boxes or notecards with dots, children take the whole word and break it into its individual sounds. As they segment the word, they tap each dot or box and pronounce each sound in order.

This activity allows teachers to monitor children's ability to segment words into individual sounds by starting with two-sound words and gradually increasing the number of sounds.

Cognitively, children must break a word apart into its individual phonemes, so they need sufficient practice with isolating single sounds before they can successfully segment an entire word. Teachers should also select words that include the target sound because this reduces cognitive load. Children are already becoming attuned to identifying that sound, and the task becomes slightly more demanding by adding one or two additional sounds that must also be held in memory and pronounced in sequence.

Children at all levels can participate in this activity, but the level of difficulty should be adjusted. Children who are still developing their skills will need repeated practice with two-sound words, especially words that contain familiar phonemes (e.g., at, it, is, as). As children become more proficient, teachers can introduce three-sound words. Children at higher levels are able to retrieve and produce more phonemes in sequence, allowing them to segment longer words more efficiently.

### [chop_the_sound] Chop the Sound

Children are given words that contain the target sound. They remove (or "chop") either the target sound or another previously learned sound and say what remains of the word.

For example, if the target sound is /b/, children may be given the word bed and asked to chop the /b/. The teacher asks, "What's the word now?" The answer is "ed."

Children can also remove other previously learned sounds. For example, if the target sound is /b/, children might hear the word bat and be asked to chop the /t/. The remaining sounds are /b/ /a/ ("ba").

As children develop more advanced phoneme awareness, this activity can also be used with blends. For example, if the blend /bl/ has been introduced, children might hear the word blend and be asked to chop the /b/, leaving "lend."

This activity supports children's ability to flexibly manipulate phonemes to determine what remains of a spoken word after a sound is removed.

Manipulating phonemes is more difficult than identifying, pronouncing, isolating, or discriminating between sounds. To complete this task, children must first segment the word into its individual sounds in order to isolate the sound that will be removed. Next, they must omit that sound and then blend the remaining sounds together to form the new word.

Because this task involves several cognitive steps, it should not be used when children are first being introduced to sounds. Children need ample practice with isolating, pronouncing, discriminating, and segmenting sounds before moving to phoneme manipulation.

This activity is most appropriate for more advanced students who have had sufficient practice with earlier phonological skills. Most children in prekindergarten are typically not yet ready for this level of phoneme manipulation. In kindergarten, some advanced children—particularly those who have had prior opportunities to develop strong sound–symbol knowledge and early segmentation skills—may be able to perform this task. On-level kindergarteners may not reach this level until later in the year. By first grade, most children should be able to complete these tasks, though the amount of support needed may vary depending on their skill level.

### [sound_switch_a_roo] Sound Switch-a-Roo

Children are given words that include previously learned sounds. They are asked to change the beginning, middle, or ending sound by substituting in the new target sound. Children may also be given a word that includes the new target sound and asked to replace it with a previously learned sound. Examples for teachers (target sound /f/): Beginning sound substitution:Teacher: "Say bat. Now change the /b/ to /f/. What's the new word?"Children: fat Middle sound substitution: Teacher: "Say fat. Now change the /a/ to /i/. What's the new word?" Children: fit. Ending sound substitution: Teacher: "Say cab. Now change the /b/ to /f/. What's the new word?" Children: caf (the teacher can note that sometimes the new word may be a silly or nonsense word)

This activity supports children's ability to flexibly manipulate phonemes to create a new spoken word by removing one sound and replacing it with another.

Manipulating phonemes is more difficult than identifying, pronouncing, isolating, or discriminating between sounds. To complete this task, children must first segment the word into its individual sounds in order to isolate the sound that will be removed. Next, they must omit that sound, insert a new sound, and then blend the remaining sounds together to form the new word.

This task involves an additional cognitive step beyond Chop the Sound, making it one of the most difficult phonological awareness tasks. Because of this complexity, it should not be used when children are first being introduced to sounds. Children need ample practice with isolating, pronouncing, discriminating, and segmenting sounds before moving to phoneme manipulation.

This activity is most appropriate for more advanced students who have had sufficient practice with earlier phonological skills. Most children in prekindergarten are typically not yet ready for this level of phoneme manipulation. In kindergarten, some advanced children—particularly those who have had prior opportunities to develop strong sound–symbol knowledge and early segmentation skills—may be able to perform this task. On-level kindergarteners may not reach this level until later in the year. By first grade, most children should be able to complete these tasks, though the amount of support needed may vary depending on their skill level.

## Handwriting Focus (5-10 min)

Order of difficulty: Finger Run, SkyWrite, FingerTrace, Magic Finger Write, Trace & Say, Start Dot & Say, Write the Sound Challenge, Cover & Try, Disappearing Letter, Letter Sprint

### [finger_run] Finger Run

Children run their finger from left to right across the handwriting lines, pointing to the top, middle, and bottom lines as the teacher names them.

This activity supports children's early understanding of concepts of print, including writing from left to right and orienting their writing within lines, which helps build the foundation for later writing fluency and accuracy.

This activity is most appropriate for children who are just beginning to write, typically in prekindergarten. However, children in early kindergarten may also need this practice if they did not receive sufficient exposure in prekindergarten. Providing these early concepts-of-print activities in prekindergarten and early kindergarten can be especially helpful for children who may struggle with early writing skills. In most cases, these activities are not necessary for first grade, as children are generally expected to have already developed these foundational writing behaviors.

### [skywrite] SkyWrite

Children write letters in the air while saying the strokes aloud, using shoulders and elbows to build motor memory. (low-level)

This activity helps children learn the correct letter formation and directionality of strokes before writing with a pencil inside lines. It serves as a cognitive scaffold because it focuses children's attention on the sequence and direction of strokes without the added demand of staying within lines. At the same time, it incorporates large motor movement, which can help reinforce memory for the stroke pattern. Sky writing is generally easier than Magic Finger Write or Finger Trace, which require greater precision and control.

### [finger_trace] FingerTrace

Children trace letter cards that include arrows showing the correct sequence of strokes. These cards can be made on notecards or created as textured cards (e.g., sandpaper or raised paint). Children trace the letters with their fingers, following the arrows to practice the correct stroke path and letter formation.

This activity helps children increase their precision and memory for the correct sequence of strokes when forming letters. It removes the added coordination required for pencil control, allowing children to focus on learning the stroke order and letter formation. As a result, it serves as a primer for writing letters within the lines when children begin using a pencil.

### [magic_finger_write] Magic Finger Write

Children use their "magic" pointer finger to trace letters within the lines while following the correct sequence of strokes. The teacher first models how to form the letter and provides verbal stroke cues, and children then trace the letter with their finger while following the teacher's guidance. This activity helps children learn the correct letter formation and directionality of strokes before writing with a pencil.

This activity helps children learn the correct letter formation and directionality of strokes before writing with a pencil. It eases cognitive load because it removes the demands of fine motor coordination, allowing children to focus on learning and remembering the sequence of strokes. As a result, the activity serves as a primer for writing within the lines when children begin writing with a pencil.

This activity is most appropriate for children who are just beginning to write, typically in prekindergarten. However, children in early kindergarten may also need this practice if they did not receive sufficient exposure in prekindergarten. Providing these early concepts-of-print activities in prekindergarten and early kindergarten can be especially helpful for children who may struggle with early writing skills. In most cases, these activities are not necessary for first grade, as children are generally expected to have already developed these foundational writing behaviors.

### [trace_and_say] Trace & Say

Children trace letters on dotted-line handwriting templates using a pencil while saying the stroke cues aloud (e.g., "start at the top, down, bump, back up…"). After tracing the strokes, children say the sound of the letter they are tracing.

This routine helps reinforce both correct letter formation and the connection between the grapheme and its corresponding phoneme. As children trace the letter, they say the sound the letter represents, linking the motor movement of writing with the sound of the letter.

The activity provides support by allowing children to practice the correct sequence of strokes using a traced model, which reduces the demands of fine motor coordination and memory. Because the letter form is already provided, children can focus on learning the stroke order and reinforcing the sound–symbol connection.

This routine works well for children in prekindergarten and for struggling kindergarteners who may need additional support with motor coordination, letter formation, and remembering the sound associated with each letter.

### [start_dot_and_say] Start Dot & Say

Children write letters on handwriting lines that include a start dot to show where to begin the letter. The start dot helps guide children to initiate the letter in the correct location and follow the proper sequence of strokes. As they write the letter, children say the stroke cues aloud (e.g., "start at the dot, down, curve…"). After forming the letter, children say the sound the letter represents.

This routine supports children in learning correct letter formation while reinforcing the connection between the grapheme and its corresponding phoneme.

This routine is one step more difficult than Trace and Say because children must write the letter themselves rather than tracing a model. A start dot is provided to show where to begin the letter, helping children initiate the correct stroke sequence. As they write the letter, children say the stroke cues and then the sound the letter represents.

Although the start dot provides guidance, children must now recall and execute the full sequence of strokes on their own, which increases the cognitive and motor demands. They must coordinate remembering the letter formation, controlling the pencil, and producing the sound of the letter at the same time. Because the full letter is no longer traced, this routine requires greater independence in both handwriting and sound–symbol recall, making it a natural step forward from tracing activities.

### [write_the_sound_challenge] Write the Sound Challenge

Children write and say the sequenced cues of their target letter/ letter patterns using correct letter formation without tracing or start-dot support. As they write, the teacher monitors the accuracy of the stroke sequence and how well the letter is formed within the lines. Children earn a star or check for each correctly formed letter.

This activity provides accuracy practice as children write letters using proper stroke order and correct placement within the lines during guided writing practice. The focus is on helping children apply what they have learned about letter formation and handwriting conventions while writing independently.

This activity is most appropriate as children develop greater working memory capacity and motor control. After successful practice with tracing and start-dot routines, children should be able to write the target letter independently following teacher modeling. Some children may still benefit from one or two traced examples on the page before writing the remaining letters on their own.

Children in prekindergarten and some struggling kindergarteners may need additional time with tracing and start-dot activities before they are ready for this level of independent writing. The Star Challenge encourages children to focus on precision and correct formation, helping them build accuracy before increasing speed or writing more complex words.

### [cover_and_try] Cover & Try

Children first trace a model letter, paying attention to the correct sequence of strokes and where the letter sits within the lines. After tracing, they cover the model and attempt to write the letter independently in the next space.

his activity supports both fluency and accuracy in letter writing. Children first trace a model letter, paying attention to the correct stroke order and placement within the lines. They then cover the model and attempt to write the letter independently in the next space.

This activity is easier than Disappearing Letters and Letter Sprint because children have just physically traced the letter, which reinforces the motor pattern and stroke sequence immediately before writing it from memory. The model is also removed only briefly, so the memory demand is lower. As a result, children can focus on reproducing the correct letter formation and placement within the lines while still benefiting from the recent sensory and motor experience of tracing.

At the same time, this activity is more difficult than Write the Sound because children must work from multiple letter models and reproduce the full letter form independently, rather than simply writing a single dictated sound. This increases the demand on both motor planning and memory for correct letter

### [disappearing_letter] Disappearing Letter

In this activity, children study a model of a letter that shows the correct sequence and starting points of each stroke. The teacher guides children to identify the letter name, the number of strokes, the type of strokes, and where each stroke begins. Children are asked to carefully study the model and keep the memory of how to form the letter in their minds.

When the teacher says "disappear," children cover the model letter. After a short pause, the teacher asks them to write the letter from memory in the next space on their worksheet. With each new attempt, the model letters remain covered and the teacher increases the wait time, requiring children to rely more on memory to recall the correct letter formation.

This activity helps strengthen children's memory for the sequence and direction of letter strokes. Children first study a model of the letter that shows the correct stroke order and starting points. When the model is covered or "disappears," children must recall the letter from memory and write it independently.

Because the visual support is gradually removed, the activity encourages children to pay close attention to where strokes begin, the order of the strokes, and the overall letter formation. This strengthens both working memory for letter patterns and independence in handwriting, as children practice writing the letter accurately without relying on a visual model.

### [letter_sprint] Letter Sprint

Children write letters, sound patterns, or simple words quickly and accurately within a short timed interval (e.g., 30–60 seconds). The teacher provides a target letter, pattern, or set of words (for example, words with the target sound such as fan, fit, fog). Children write as many correct letters or words as they can within the time limit while maintaining correct letter formation, stroke sequence, and placement within the lines. After time ends, the teacher reviews the writing with students, checking both accuracy and quantity. Students can count how many letters or words were written correctly and track their improvement over time.

This activity is appropriate for more advanced learners because it requires children to retrieve letter forms or spellings quickly while maintaining accuracy. By this stage, children should already have strong knowledge of letters and letter formation, allowing them to focus on building automaticity and writing fluency rather than learning the basic mechanics of letter formation.

## Spelling Focus (5-10 min)

Order of Difficulty: Trace the Sound, Write the Sound, Sound-Letter Hunt, Stretch-and-Write, Switch-A-Roo Write, Sentence Challenge, Spell Sprint

### [trace_the_sound] Trace the Sound

The teacher says a series of sounds that include the target sound multiple times (e.g., /m/, /z/, /a/, /m/). Each time children hear the target sound, they trace the corresponding letter or letter pattern on their handwriting sheet.

Same cognitive support as Sound Check, adding in writing the letter of matching target sound

This activity supports children in connecting the sound they hear with its written representation while keeping the task manageable. Because children are tracing rather than writing independently, the cognitive demand is lower. The tracing also reinforces correct letter formation and stroke sequence while children practice recognizing the sound.

This is considered a low-level activity because the teacher provides the sound and the written letter model, allowing children to focus on sound recognition and sound–symbol mapping without needing to retrieve the letter form from memory.

### [write_the_sound_spelling] Write the Sound

The teacher says a sound (e.g., /m/ or /sh/), and children write the letter or letter pattern that represents that sound. Children write on a handwriting sheet that shows the correct formation of previously learned letters along with the new target letter, but there is no tracing provided.

Same cognitive support as Tap the Sound and Sound Sparks but children write the correct corresponding letter.

This activity helps children connect phoneme recognition with independent letter writing. Because the teacher provides the sound and children only need to retrieve and write the corresponding letter or letter pattern, the cognitive demand remains manageable. The handwriting sheet serves as a visual reference for correct letter formation, helping children practice accurate writing within the lines while strengthening the sound–symbol connection.

This is considered a low-level activity because children are focusing primarily on matching a sound to its written form, rather than segmenting or manipulating sounds within words

### [sound_letter_hunt] Sound-Letter Hunt

The teacher says a word, and children listen carefully to determine whether the target sound occurs at the beginning, middle, or end of the word. After identifying where they hear the sound, children write the corresponding letter (grapheme) in that position.

To support writing, children can use a handwriting sheet that displays previously learned letters along with the new target letter, showing the correct formation and placement within the lines. This helps reinforce both sound–symbol connections and accurate handwriting. For younger children in prekindergarten, teachers can provide dotted-line letters next to the target letter so children can trace before writing independently.

Same Cognitive Support as Sound Hunt, but children identify by writing the letter.

This activity strengthens children's ability to connect phoneme identification with spelling, requiring them to listen for the sound, determine its position in the word, and then represent that sound in writing. It is considered a middle-level task because children must integrate phoneme awareness with grapheme recall and handwriting.

### [stretch_and_write] Stretch-and-Write

The teacher says a word, and children stretch the word into its individual sounds. Using Elkonin boxes, children segment the sounds they hear and write the letter that represents each sound in the corresponding box.

Example: Teacher says map. Children stretch the word /m/ /a/ /p/ and write m – a – p in the boxes.

This activity can be modified to provide different levels of support and complexity.

Level 1 – Initial Sound Focus (Either/Or Choice) Children stretch the word but focus only on the beginning sound. The Elkonin boxes already include the middle and ending letters, and children choose between two possible beginning consonants. Example: The boxes show _at. The teacher says bat or cat. Children stretch the word and choose whether the beginning letter is b or c.

Level 2 – CVC Words with Known Rimes Children segment and spell CVC words, but the ending pattern (rime) has already been taught. Children know that when they hear the ending /at/, the word will end with at. Example words: cat, sat, bat, mat, hat. Children stretch the word and write the beginning sound along with a known rime to complete the spelling.

Level 3 – Multiple Ending Patterns Children segment and spell CVC words that include different rime patterns that have been previously taught (e.g., –at, –an, –it, –op). This requires children to listen more carefully to identify both the vowel and final consonant. Example words: cat, can, sit, mop.

Level 4 – Blends, Digraphs, and More Complex Patterns Children stretch and spell words that include consonant blends, digraphs, or more complex sound patterns. Examples: ship, clap, frog, thin, live, shame. At this level, children must segment and represent multiple consonants or letter patterns while maintaining correct sound–symbol mapping.

Same as Stretch-the-Sounds with added letter write

Appropriate when modified in Kindergarten, increasing intensity through 1st grade.

### [switch_a_roo_write] Switch-A-Roo Write

In this activity, children replace one sound in a word with the target sound and then write the new word. The teacher first has children write a word, then asks them to change one sound and write the new word that results. This requires children to segment the original word, identify the sound to be replaced, substitute the new sound, and then spell the new word.

Example (target sound /f/): Teacher: "Write bat. Now change /b/ to /f/." → Children write fat. Teacher: "Write fat. Now change /a/ to /i/." → Children write fit.

This activity develops children's ability to flexibly manipulate phonemes while encoding them in writing, which supports both spelling and decoding.

The activity can be modified to provide different levels of support and complexity.

Level 1 – Beginning Sound Change Children change only the initial consonant of a word while the rest of the word stays the same. This is the easiest level because children only need to focus on the first sound. Example: Teacher: "Write bat. Now change /b/ to /f/." → fat Teacher: "Write mat. Now change /m/ to /f/." → fat

Level 2 – Ending Sound Change Children change the final sound of a word while keeping the beginning sounds the same. Example: Teacher: "Write fan. Now change /n/ to /t/." → fat Teacher: "Write fit. Now change /t/ to /n/." → fin

Level 3 – Middle Sound Change (Vowel Substitution) Children replace the middle vowel sound, which requires more precise listening and segmentation. Example: Teacher: "Write fat. Now change /a/ to /i/." → fit Teacher: "Write fit. Now change /i/ to /a/." → fat

Level 4 – Complex Patterns (Blends, Digraphs, or Multiple Changes) Children replace sounds in words that include blends, digraphs, or more complex patterns. Example: Teacher: "Write flat. Now change /fl/ to /sl/." → slat Teacher: "Write ship. Now change /sh/ to /f/." → fip (the teacher can note when a word may be a nonsense word).

At higher levels, children must hold the word in working memory, manipulate the sound structure, and accurately encode the new spelling, making this a more advanced spelling task.

### [sentence_challenge] Sentence Challenge

In this activity, children think of words that contain the target sound or a target rime pattern and then write a sentence using as many of those words as possible. The teacher first reviews the target sound or pattern and helps children brainstorm possible words. Children then compose and write a sentence, trying to include several of the target words.

Example (target rime /at/): Children might write: The fat cat sat on the mat.

Example (target sound /s/): Children might write: Sam sees six snakes in the sand.

A little more difficult than Silly Sentence because children need to use words that contain spelling patterns they have learned.

This activity strengthens children's ability to retrieve words from memory that share a sound or spelling pattern and apply them in writing. It also integrates phonics, spelling, vocabulary retrieval, and sentence construction, helping children practice encoding sounds while building simple sentences.

### [spell_sprint] Spell Sprint

In this activity, the teacher dictates words that contain previously taught sounds or spelling patterns, and children write each word as quickly and accurately as possible. The goal is to build spelling fluency while maintaining correct letter formation and accurate spelling.

The teacher says a word and then begins a countdown to the next word. For example, the teacher may say the word and give students 10 seconds to write it. As students become more fluent, the time between words can be reduced to 6 seconds, and eventually to 3 seconds. After each countdown, the teacher says the next word and students immediately write it.

Example routine: Teacher: "The word is cat. Write cat." Teacher counts: "10… 9… 8…" (continuing until the next word). Teacher: "Next word: sat."

After the activity, the teacher reviews the words with the class and checks for correct spelling, accurate letter formation, and writing within the lines.

This activity encourages children to retrieve sound–symbol patterns quickly while still maintaining accuracy, helping develop both spelling automaticity and handwriting fluency.
