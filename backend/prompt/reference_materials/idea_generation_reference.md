## FORM M: Elements of the Genre

THIS IS FORM M.
Elements of Genre 

	-	Informational/Explanatory Text 
1. Informational / Explanatory Text  Children write to share real information about a topic they know or are learning about. This type of writing helps them explain facts, ideas, or how something works.  Example: Writing about animals that live in the ocean or how pumpkins grow or the life cycle of a butterfly.
Elements of Genre to teach to support idea generation and organization
Writing a topic sentence (Main Idea) = Topic + What is the main thing you want to explain about the topic (The bodies of polar bears help them live in the Artic)
Relevant & Irrelevant Details = details that explain and elaborate the topic. (Polar bears have thick fur to keep them warm vs. Penguins are good swimmers) 
Fact vs. Fantasy = facts that are real and observable not make believe. (Polar bears have thick fur to keep them warm vs. Polar bears go to school and play with their friends at recess).
Structure = Main Idea + supporting details + Conclusion 
2. Reading Response (Summary / Recall) Children respond to a book they read or heard by telling what it was about. This may include retelling key events. Example: After reading The Very Hungry Caterpillar, they write or draw to show what happened first, next, and last.
Elements of Genre to teach to support idea generation and organization
Characters & Setting = identifying who the characters are and where are they 
Textual Evidence = getting kids to understand that they are summarizing what the text says not what they make up on their own. 
Sequence of events = identifying chronological order, what happened 1st, 2nd, and 3rd, etc. 
Problem & Resolution = what was the main problem/conflict of the story and how did the problem get solved.
Structure = Beginning, Middle, and End = identify main details of a story in order. 
3. Compare and Contrast  Children look at two things and describe how they are the same and how they are different. This builds thinking and language skills.  Example: Writing about how mammals and fish are alike and different, or comparing two characters from a story.
Elements of Genre to teach to support idea generation and organization
Writing a Main Idea topic sentence = Topic + What is the main thing you want to explain about the topic (Polar bears and Black bears are different types of bears)
Relevant & Irrelevant Details = details that explain and elaborate the topic. (Polar bears have thick fur to keep them warm vs. Penguins are good swimmers) 
Same supporting details vs. Different supporting details 
Fact vs. Fantasy = facts that are real and observable not make believe. (Polar bears have thick fur to keep them warm vs. Polar bears go to school and play with their friends at recess).
Structure =Topic Sentence (Main idea), supporting details about similarities and differences, conclusion  
	-	Opinion/Argumentative Writing    
1. Personal Opinion Writing  Children share what they like or think and give a reason why. This type of writing encourages them to express preferences or beliefs.  Example: “Recess is my favorite part of school because I can play with my friends. Friends make the day fun. 
Elements of Genre to teach to support idea generation and organization
Writing a topic sentence (Thesis) = Topic + What is the main thing you believe or think about that topic (Polar bears are amazing animals.)
Opinion vs Fact = understanding the difference between a statement that expresses an opinion or belief or conclusion about a topic and a statement that explains a fact. (My favorite mammal is the manatee vs. Manatees float to the surface of the water in order to breathe.)
Relevant & Irrelevant Details = details that explain and elaborate the topic. (Polar bears have thick fur to keep them warm vs. Penguins are good swimmers) 
Structure = Thesis Statement/Opinion Statement  + supporting details (Why?) + Conclusion (the importance of the why to the writer) 
2. Problem–Solution Writing (Cause and Effect Thinking) This is an early version of problem-solution writing. Children describe a problem and imagine a helpful solution. Exploring a problem helps them think through events and how one thing leads to another.  Example: “Littering can cause animals to get hurt. When people throw trash on the ground, animals might think it’s food or get stuck in it. That can make them sick or hurt. We should throw out our trash.”
Elements of Genre to teach to support idea generation and organization
Writing a statement of the problem (Thesis) = Topic + What is the main thing you believe or think about that topic (Ice melting in the arctic can cause polar bears to go extinct).
Opinion vs Fact = understanding the difference between a statement that expresses an opinion or belief or conclusion about a topic and a statement that explains a fact.
Relevant & Irrelevant Details = details that explain and elaborate the topic. (Polar bears have thick fur to keep them warm vs. Penguins are good swimmers) 
Structure = Statement of Problem + Effect of Problem (how does the problem lead to something not good) + Conclusion: Solution to the Problem 

4. Reading Response (Inferring / Drawing Conclusions)  Children think beyond what the text says to explain why something happened or what a character might be thinking or feeling. They use clues from the story to support their ideas.  Example: “I think the boy was sad because he lost his toy. In the text it says, he put his head down, and he’s not smiling.” No one likes to lose a toy.” 
Elements of Genre to teach to support idea generation and organization
Writing a thesis statement for reading response = Topic (answer to the question, e.g., Why is the boy sad = The boy is sad because ..) + What is the main thing you want to explain about the topic (e.g., because he lost his toy).
Cause & Effect Opinion: Why –what would be the effects of a textual event / what are the causes of a textual event.
Relevant & Irrelevant Details = details that explain and elaborate the topic. (Polar bears have thick fur to keep them warm vs. Penguins are good swimmers) 
Textual Evidence = What the text directly says 
Inference = what is the meaning of the text, considering what the text says AND what we know about life in general.
Reading Response Opinion Structure = Answer the Question in a complete statement, textual evidence, Conclusion (connect to what we know in general).

	-	Narrative
1. Personal Narrative  Children write or tell a true story about something that happened to them. It often includes where they were, what they did, and how they felt. This helps build a sense of time, sequence, and self-expression.  Example: “I went to the park. I saw a big dog. I felt scared, but it was nice. I didn’t need to be scared”
Elements of Genre to teach to support idea generation and organization
Writing an exposition = Introduce Character Setting and Problem
Cause and Effect = Rising Action (e.g., how does the main character (the writer) react to the problem?)
Sequence of Events = identifying chronological order, what happened 1st, 2nd, and 3rd, etc.
Conclusion = What did the writer come to understand 
2. Story Writing (Fictional Narrative)
Children make up a story with characters and events. These stories can be realistic or imaginary. Story writing helps develop creativity, language, and understanding of story structure. Example: “Once there was a dragon who loved cupcakes. One day, he ran out, so he flew to the bakery!”
Elements of Genre to teach to support idea generation and organization
Writing an exposition = Introduce Character Setting and Problem
Cause and Effect = Rising Action (e.g., how does the main character react to the problem?)
Sequence of Events = identifying chronological order, what happened 1st, 2nd, and 3rd, etc.
Dialogue = what characters would say to move the plot forward  
Conclusion = How does the problem get resolved 
Descriptive Writing = Sensory details to create tone for readers to feel a mood.

Suggested Activities For Idea Generation & Organization  


Review & Connect Activities 

All of these activities would be appropriate for all levels. The content of discussions and complexity of the skill will increase as children advance in their writing skills. 
Concept Picture Walk: Children revisit key images from a shared text, anchor chart, or concept map. The teacher guides students to recall facts using questions like “What do you see?” or “What do you remember?” Children generate ideas by connecting pictures to unit concepts. This supports idea generation by activating background knowledge through visuals and guided discussion.
Video Clip + Think-Pair-Share: Children view a short video clip related to the unit topic and connected to texts being read (e.g., penguins in the cold). The teacher pauses to ask targeted questions that prompt children to think and talk with a partner. This helps them recall facts, build vocabulary, and connect new information to class learning.
Genre Song or Chant (to the tune of “If You’re Happy and You Know It”)  Children sing a repetitive, upbeat chant set to the familiar melody of "If You're Happy and You Know It." For example, for informational writing, the chant could be:
🎵  “If it’s real and it’s true, it’s a fact (clap, clap)  If it teaches something new, it’s a fact (clap, clap)  If you’re writing to explain,  And your facts are super plain,  Then you’re working in the information lane!” (clap, clap)  🎵
The teacher pairs the chant with visuals (e.g., a picture of a nonfiction book, animals, or diagrams) and gestures (e.g., pointing to the brain for "think," clapping for rhythm, miming writing or reading). This multisensory approach helps children internalize the purpose and structure of the genre through rhythm, movement, and repetition.
Home-to-School Sharing Circle: Children connect personal or family experiences to the topic of study (e.g., sharing what they wear in winter). The teacher helps children link these experiences to the genre and unit content (e.g., “Just like you wear coats, penguins have feathers to stay warm.”). This builds relevance and supports idea development.
Genre Poster Sort: Children help sort sentence strips or images into categories (e.g., informational or narrative) based on what they learned about the features of each genre. This supports genre recognition and reinforces the kind of writing children will produce.
 Model, Explain, Engage Activities  
Follow the below format for this section of the lesson plan.
First explain or define in easy to understand terms the skill or concept in easy to understand terms 
If appropriate, provide examples and non-examples of the concept.    
Explain and Engage means that the teacher provides explicit definitions and detailed examples to demonstrate a targeted skill.
Examples are important and should be precise in demonstrating a concept or skill in order to dispel misconceptions. (e.g., what is and is not a topic sentence/ what is or is not a relevant detail) Use Engleman Theory of Learning and PDFs The Power of Example, The Components of Direct Instruction and The Components of Direct Instruction 2,when providing examples and non-examples.
An example of applying Engelmann’s theory of learning, particularly from Direct Instruction (DI), to teaching cohesive devices, you would focus on the clear presentation of a concept using:
	-	Positive (yes) examples: where the concept is present. 
	-	Negative (no) or nonexamples: where the concept is absent or violated. 
	-	Consistent language and minimal differences between examples and nonexamples so students can more easily discriminate the defining feature (i.e., the cohesive device and how it works to link ideas). 
Below is how you might apply Engelmann’s approach to cohesive devices:
🔹 Concept: Cohesive devices link ideas and sentences so that writing flows and ideas connect logically.
✅ Examples (YES examples):
These show clear, appropriate use of cohesive devices (e.g., because, but, and, then, also, so).
	-	"Penguins have thick feathers, so they stay warm in cold places."  → Shows causal connection with so. 
	-	"Penguins swim fast, but they waddle on land."  → Contrast is clearly marked with but. 
	-	"First, the penguin dives into the water. Then, it catches a fish."*  → Sequence is linked with first and then. 
	-	"Penguins have flippers. Also, they have webbed feet."  → Additional information is added using also. 

❌ Nonexamples (NO examples):
These either omit the cohesive device or misuse it in a way that breaks the connection between ideas.
	-	"Penguins have thick feathers. They stay warm in cold places."  → No linking word; ideas are related but not explicitly connected. 
	-	"Penguins swim fast. They waddle on land."  → Contrast is not made clear without a device like but or however. 
	-	"The penguin catches a fish. The penguin dives into the water."  → The sequence is reversed and lacks words like first, then, or after to show order. 
	-	"Penguins have flippers. They live in the snow."  → No clear connection between ideas; lacking a cohesive device that links function to habitat (e.g., because or so).

(Level 1):  Children are shown a simple topic sentence (e.g., “Polar bears are strong animals”) and two possible next sentences: one that clearly connects (e.g., “They use their big paws to walk on ice”) and one that is clearly off-topic (e.g., “Some birds migrate to warm places”). (Level 2):  Children are given a topic sentence (e.g., “Arctic foxes survive by blending into their surroundings”) and two closely related next-sentence options: one that logically builds the idea (e.g., “Their white fur hides them in the snow from predators”) and one that is loosely related but less focused (e.g., “They eat small animals like lemmings”). Children discuss which detail best supports the topic and why, encouraging precision in idea development. (middle)
Which Sentence Builds the Topic? (Level 3): Children are shown a topic sentence and three possible next sentences that vary in how directly they build on the idea. With teacher support, they are guided to evaluate the options, select the sentence that most clearly supports or expands on the topic sentence, and justify their reasoning (e.g., “This one connects because it tells how the hare hides”). Circle the Cohesion – Words That Connect Level 1: Early Awareness of Connecting Words  Children are presented with a short, teacher-written sentence pair or very simple paragraph using basic cohesive words (e.g., and, but, then). The teacher reads the text aloud with clear expression and models how these words help ideas stick together. Using a shared chart or sentence strip, the teacher prompts children to circle or highlight each cohesive word together as a group. As each word is identified, the teacher explains its function using visuals or gestures (e.g., “Then tells us what happened next”).  Example:  Text: “The penguin slid on the ice, and it splashed into the water.”  Prompt: “Let’s circle the word that sticks these two actions together. What does and help us know?”  (low)
Circle the Cohesion – Words That Connect Level 2: Identify and Compare Types of Cohesion  Children work with a short modeled paragraph (3–4 sentences) that includes different types of cohesive devices, such as addition (also), contrast (but), cause (because), and time (then, after). After the teacher reads the text aloud, children work with a partner or as a class to circle each cohesive word and discuss its role in connecting the ideas. The teacher prompts children to explain how these words help the sentences make sense as a whole.  Example:  Text: “Penguins huddle together to stay warm. Also, they have thick feathers. They can still get cold because the wind is strong.”  Prompt: “Which word shows us a new idea that goes with the first? Which word tells us why they get cold?”  (middle-high)
Circle the Cohesion – Words That Connect Level 3: Evaluate and Revise for Cohesion  Children are given a teacher-written paragraph that includes some strong cohesive devices and some weak or missing connections. After reading the paragraph aloud, students work in pairs or small groups to identify and underline existing cohesive devices, discuss their function, and then revise the paragraph by adding or improving cohesive words. They justify their choices by explaining how the added words help the ideas flow.  Example:  Original Text: “Penguins dive into the water. They catch fish. The water is cold. They swim fast.”  Student Revision: “Penguins dive into the water. Then, they catch fish. Although the water is cold, they swim fast.”  Prompt: “Where could we add a word to help the ideas connect more smoothly? Why does that word help?”  (high)
Build the Flow – Rearranging for Cohesion Level 1: One Idea, Right Order Children are presented with a pair of related sentences or sentence parts that are jumbled. The teacher reads the parts aloud and models thinking: “Which sentence comes first? Which one adds on?” Using visuals or movement (e.g., sentence strips on a pocket chart), children rearrange the sentence parts so the second idea clearly builds upon the first. The teacher emphasizes how one sentence leads to the next using cohesive logic (e.g., time, cause, or contrast).  Example:  Jumbled: “He is cold. The penguin jumps into the icy water.”  Corrected: “The penguin jumps into the icy water. He is cold.”  Prompt: “Which one tells what happened first? Which one explains the result?” (low)
Build the Flow – Rearranging for Cohesion Level 2: Short Paragraph Sequencing with Logical Connections  Children are given 3–4 mixed-up sentences about a familiar topic. Working with a partner or in a small group, they read the sentences aloud, then rearrange them in the order that makes the most sense. The teacher prompts students to look for clues like time words (first, then), cause/effect (because, so), or comparisons (but, also) to guide sequencing. Children read their revised paragraph aloud and explain how one idea builds on the next.  Example (jumbled): The penguin dives deep under the water. It sees a shiny fish swimming by. It is hungry. It catches the fish in its beak. Correct Order: “The penguin is hungry. It dives deep under the water. It sees a shiny fish swimming by. It catches the fish in its beak.”  Prompt: “Which sentence gives us the reason? What happened next? What sentence brings it to a close?” (middle)
Build the Flow – Rearranging for Cohesion Level 3: Fill the Gaps – Writing Cohesive Links Children are given a paragraph with key cohesive elements missing. Some sentences are in the correct order, but the logical flow between them is unclear. Students are tasked with adding their own connecting phrases or transitional words (e.g., First, Then, Because of this, Although) in the blank spaces to improve cohesion. They can also revise sentence structure for clarity and better flow. Afterward, they read their version aloud and explain how their additions help the ideas connect.  Example:  Text with blanks:  “The penguin is cold. ___________, it dives into the water to catch fish. ___________, it uses its wings to swim fast. It eats the fish quickly. ___________, it pops back up onto the ice.”  Student Fill-in Example:  “The penguin is cold. Still, it dives into the water to catch fish. Then, it uses its wings to swim fast. It eats the fish quickly. After that, it pops back up onto the ice.”  Prompt: “What words can we add so the story feels smoother? How do your words help us understand the penguin’s actions?” (high)
Fact vs. Fantasy – Distinguishing Real from Make-Believe Level 1: Two Choices – What’s Real? At this beginning level, the teacher introduces the concept of real versus pretend information using vivid examples and concrete language. Children are shown two choices at a time—either through images, sentence cards, or oral storytelling. One option reflects a factual detail about a real-world animal, place, or process, and the other represents an imaginative or fantasy element (e.g., talking animals, magical events). The teacher reads both aloud, then prompts:  “Which one could really happen in the world? Which one is just for fun or make-believe?” Children point, circle, or sort the correct choice with guidance.Example: “A penguin swims underwater to catch fish.” ✅ “A penguin uses a magic wand to fly in the sky.” ❌ The teacher explains why the first detail is based on real-life animal behavior, while the second is make-believe. This activity may be done in small group or circle time with picture support. (low)
Fact vs. Fantasy – Distinguishing Real from Make-Believe Level 2: Sort the Details – Fact or Fantasy?  Children are given a mixed set of 6–8 sentence or image cards related to a specific topic (e.g., “How animals survive in the cold” or “What happens in the ocean”). Some statements are grounded in real-world facts, while others include fantasy elements. In groups or pairs, students sort the cards into two categories: Fact and Fantasy. The teacher then checks with students and asks questions like: “How do you know that’s real?” or “What makes this one sound pretend?” Example Card Set: “A polar bear builds a den in the snow.” ✅“A snowflake talks to a baby seal.” ❌ “Walruses rest together on ice.” ✅ “Penguins wear winter hats and scarves.” ❌(middle)
Fact vs. Fantasy – Distinguishing Real from Make-Believe Level 3: Revise the Fantasy – Make It Real  At this level, children work with sentences that contain imaginative or fantasy details, and their task is to revise or rewrite the sentences to make them factually accurate. The teacher first models one or two examples, showing how to replace silly or magical parts with real-world information. Then children are given 3–5 sentences containing imaginative elements and prompted to “make them real” using what they know about the topic. Example: Original: “The penguin danced with a snowman at the North Pole.” Revised: “The penguin slid on the ice to find its food.” Children are encouraged to use content knowledge, shared texts, or classroom anchor charts to support revision. The teacher may prompt: “What part is make-believe? What could happen instead?” (high)
Level 1: Two Choices – What’s a Fact? What’s an Opinion?  At this introductory level, the teacher presents children with two simple sentences at a time—one factual and one opinion-based—paired with visuals and read aloud in a shared format. The teacher models the difference using sentence stems such as “A fact is something that is true and can be proven” and “An opinion is how someone feels or what they like.” Children are prompted to choose which sentence is the fact and which is the opinion, often by pointing, circling, or sorting with teacher support. For example: “Penguins live in cold places” (fact) vs. “Penguins are the cutest animals” (opinion). This level focuses on recognition with guided discussion and clear cues to help children distinguish between what is true for everyone and what is a personal feeling. (low)
Level 2: Sort Multiple Sentences – Fact or Opinion?  At this level, children are given a set of 6–8 short sentences and work independently or in small groups to sort them into “Fact” and “Opinion” categories using a pocket chart, sentence strips, or individual paper sort. Sentences are topic-linked (e.g., animals, weather, or food) and include both straightforward facts and clear opinions. Students may be provided with visual anchors or sentence stems such as “I think…” or “It is true that…” to help guide their decision-making. The teacher prompts students to explain how they know a sentence is a fact (e.g., “because we read it in a book” or “because it can be proven”) versus an opinion (“because people can feel differently about it”). Example sentences might include: “Walruses have tusks” (fact) vs. “Walruses look funny” (opinion). This level encourages reasoning and comparison while reinforcing academic vocabulary. (middle)
Level 3: Create or Revise – Make a Fact or Make an Opinion  At the most advanced level, children are given mixed sentences that are vague or imprecise and asked to revise them into clearer facts or stronger opinions, or to generate their own original examples of each. The teacher first models how to strengthen a sentence like “Penguins are nice” into “In my opinion, penguins are interesting because they waddle and swim.” Then children work independently or in pairs to rewrite 3–5 mixed sentences. Alternatively, they may be given a neutral sentence and asked to turn it into both a fact and an opinion version. For example: Given “Penguins live in Antarctica,” a child might write, “Fact: Penguins live in the Antarctic, where it is cold,” and “Opinion: I think penguins are the best animals in the snow.” This level emphasizes critical thinking, sentence-level writing, and clear use of evidence or personal perspective.  (high)

Sequencing – Logical Order of Events: Children receive pictures or sentence strips representing steps in a process (e.g., “How penguins hatch”) or a story sequence. They help arrange the items in the correct order, using ordinal words like first, then, and last.

What Conflict Can Come From This Setting?: Children are shown an image of a setting (e.g., icy land, stormy sea) and brainstorm with the teacher what problems could happen in that place. This supports early narrative planning.
What Conflict Can Come From This Character?: Children are shown a character (e.g., a scared penguin chick) and help predict what kind of trouble or challenge that character might face. This sparks imagination and story idea generation.
Cause and Effect Narrative Conflict: Children explore story problems through simple cause-and-effect models (e.g., “It was very cold → The penguin got lost → He needed to find shelter”). The teacher guides them to build short story chains.
Build the Suspense – Order the Intensity: Children are given a set of events with different levels of excitement or danger. They work with the teacher to order the events from least to most suspenseful, learning how to build tension in a story. (high)

## FORM T: Writing Stage


There are four writing stages: brainstorm, initial draft, revise, edit 


Principles of Instruction for brainstorming, 

1/ brainstorming has to do with retrieving relevant details. In this way, activities that support retrieval of concepts that relate to coherence (ideas related to a topic) are a first step. Typically, review and connect should briefly support child retrieval of concepts related to the writing topic.  

2/ brainstorming also has to do with understanding genre features. In this way, activities that explicitly focus on genre features, e.g., logical sequence, main idea-supporting detail, also typically in the guided practice section. 

3/ brainstorming also has to do with organization that links to genre features, such as topic sentence for informational text vs. character, setting, problem for story opening,  relevant and irrelevant details, cohesion devices and cohesion between sentences. Typically, guided practice section 

Principles of instruction for initial draft 

1/ initial draft is typically an application activity. Children should have already had direct instruction on how to write a specific part of their initial draft. Importantly, an initial draft for young children should not be in one sitting. If children learned how to write a topic sentence and had multiple guided practice opportunities, then they can begin to write their own topic sentence, applying the steps that were taught. Then children may receive instruction on revision and apply that to their initial draft of their topic sentence. This would be done first before moving on to generating relevant details to elaborate the topic. 

2/ The teacher can provide scaffolds for application activities, these can be visuals of the steps children should follow or can be scaffolds within the materials children are using, for example sentence starter, writing lines for each child-generated word if in a small group.  


Principles of Instruction for revising:

1/ Revising comes after children first attempt an initial draft. Teachers need to model the steps children are doing as they apply a specific skill/concept to revise a sentence(s). 

2/ Revising should always be focused on one specific skill as they re-generate an idea with genre in mind (organization). 

3/ Revising is not Editing. When revising children should NOT be editing as well. 

4/ Teachers can support struggling writers through revision processes in small groups. Teachers can provide prompts and scaffolds (word banks, sentence stems, visuals). Teachers can work with struggling students in small groups to help them apply but the application is more guided. More guidance means that the teacher has the child do one step, and checks it, then moves on to another step. To modify any activity, break the task down further into its component parts, according to theories of working and long-term memory. 


Principles of Instruction for editing:

1/ editing should be be focused on grammatical errors (such as subject-verb agreement), punctuation (periods, capitals), or spelling 
2/ editing is an application activity. Teachers need to first directly model and explicitly teach the skill, providing multiple opportunities of guided practice.

## Additional: The Power of Examples and Non-Examples

The Power of Examples and Non-Examples

Been re-reading Englemann and Carnine's Theory of Instruction through a lens of what we know about learning from the last 50 years and realising the brilliance of it in terms of how it incorporates so much of how learning happens. It's not an easy read but for me the core concept in it is "faultless communication": the idea that teaching should be designed so precisely that misunderstanding is impossible.
One key idea is that students learn faster when they see what something is and what it isn’t. One of the most important aspect of curriculum planning + instructional design is effectively using examples and non-examples. By that I mean cases that are deliberately chosen to contrast with examples, differing only in the critical feature that defines the concept being taught. eg/ When teaching triangles, an example might be a three-sided shape, while a non-example could be a four-sided shape—not a random object like a cloud.
One of the mad things about examples/non-examples is that it's almost a unified theory of learning in the sense that so many theorists from a range of different traditions have advocated for it in one form or another. Socrates, Aristotle, Vygotsky, Bruner, Skinner, Ausubel, Sweller all basically say the same thing on this which is that learning is driven by clear distinctions—knowing what something is requires knowing what it isn’t. It's really about concept refinement but done in an incredibly detailed way.

A key idea is that students need to see only relevant contrasts not arbitrary differences. In other words, when teaching a concept, the differences shown to a student should highlight the specific quality that defines that concept. A non-example should be almost identical to the example, differing only in the defining feature otherwise, students might focus on the wrong thing. Engelmann and Carnine developed five principles to do this:
1. The Wording Principle: To keep instruction clear, teachers should use consistent or nearly identical wording across examples. This minimises distractions and helps students focus on key details. Here, one pair maintains consistent wording, while the other introduces unnecessary variation, causing confusion.

2. The setup principle: When introducing a new concept, examples and non-examples should be as similar as possible, differing only in the critical feature. If unnecessary differences exist, students may misinterpret the rule. Figure 2.2 demonstrates this: in the incorrect example, multiple differences create ambiguity—students might mistakenly associate the concept with shape, color, or position. Without careful control of contrasts, learning becomes guesswork rather than clarity. Direct Instruction prioritises precision to eliminate confusion.

3. The Difference Principle. Positive and negative examples are most effective when they differ only slightly, but for this distinction to be clear, they must be presented consecutively. In set A, two examples are shown with minimal differences in orientation, making the contrast easy to grasp. However, in set B, the same examples appear but are separated by other items. Without direct comparison, the key difference is less obvious, and the learner may fail to recognize the defining feature. Juxtaposition ensures that critical distinctions are immediately noticeable, reinforcing accurate concept formation.

4. The sameness principle. To emphasize sameness, examples should be highly different yet share the same label. Here, various instances of "under" are shown—some erasers close to the table, others farther away, some centered, others near the edges. Despite these differences, the label remains the same, reinforcing that position changes do not alter the meaning of "under." The examples follow the wording principle (consistent phrasing), setup principle (same objects), and sameness principle (varying examples with the same label). By juxtaposing diverse cases, learners see that observed differences are irrelevant to the concept being taught.
