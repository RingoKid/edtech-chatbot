## FORM E: Language Construction Best Practices

THIS IS FORM E 

MAJOR PART 2 Vocabulary and Sentence Structure Evidence-Based Practices for best practices to model the lesson after.



Evidence-Based Practice 
Have children retrieve and use words from a unit theme within sentences related to the writing topic.
Encourage children to play with words by making connections, grouping them into categories, and exploring how they relate to the writing topic or unit theme. 
Teach children connecting or “mortar” words like "because" for cause and effect and "also" to add ideas, helping them organize and link their thoughts clearly in writing.
Explicitly and systematically focus on one language structure at a time—like subject-verb clauses or prepositional phrases–to build sentence awareness
Support children’s sentence development by having them build, rearrange, combine, and expand sentences related to the writing topic or unit theme.

2. Model, Explain, Engage (10 minutes)
[Explanation: The purpose of this section is to introduce and reinforce a targeted sentence structure through modeling and providing clear examples, while connecting to genre purpose and incorporating children’s vocabulary associated with what they are reading and writing about in their unit of study]

#Teacher Script: 
Great! So we know that a sentence needs a subject and a verb. Verbs are usually actions. But, remember we are writing informational text. Sometimes we need to tell information about our subject. Like, an Orca is a predator. {Teacher displays the sentence} 
Did the Orca do anything? (Children should respond no). 
No. we are just telling something about the Orca’s being –or something that is about who they are as a sea creature. So these are special verbs. They are not action verbs. 
They are “be” verbs: The Orca is a predator. {Teacher circles/underlines the word “is”}. 
I could also say the Orcas are predators. {Teacher circles the word are}. 
Why did I use are? (Children should respond that there is more than one.) 
Right. We use the verb are when talking about more than one person, place, thing, or animal.  
{Teacher displays the words “is” and “are”}. So, these two special be-verbs are used to tell information about the subject –the who or what we are talking about. 
Let me show you some more examples. {Teacher displays example sentences, calling children’s attention to the purpose of the sentence and the be-verb used for that purpose}. 
Sofia is a good listener. In this sentence I want to tell you something special about Sophia. She didn’t do anything. I am just telling you something about her. She’s a good listener. 
What verb did I use to tell you she’s a good listener? Think about our special be-verbs. (Children should respond “is”) 
How about this sentence: Cal and Floyd are my students. Did Cal and Floyd do anything? (Children should respond no)
No, they didn’t, but I want to tell you that they are my friends.  
What verb did I use to tell you they're my friends? Think about our special be-verbs. (Children should respond “are”) 
Why did I use “are” and not “is”? (Children should respond that there is more than one person as the subject).
Exactly. I am going to show some be-verb sentences. You tell me if the be-verb I used is “is”  or “are.”
My turn. Listen. Whales are mammals. Get Ready. What’s the verb?. (Children answer are) 
Right! I want to say that all whales are mammals, not just one. And if I am talking about more than one whale, how did I change the word “whale”? (Children should notice that there is an s on the end)
Exactly Right. When we put an s at the end of a noun, it changes it into more than one. {Teacher provides a few more examples}

Suggested Activities For Vocabulary & Sentence Structure 


Review & Connect Activities 


#Use Review & Connect Activities to respond to teacher prompting for modification of activity according to child ability level. 

Spotlight Sentence – Teacher reads or displays a sentence and asks, “Who is this sentence about?” or “What is happening?” Students identify the subject or verb aloud.  “Who did it?” and “What did they do?” (low)
Stretch It Out: The teacher shows a simple sentence. Children are given choices of the sentence structure they are reviewing and must add those sentence structures to the base sentence. For example, if focusing on delayed adjective phrases, children would choose either “scared and lonely” or “excited and happy” to add to the end of the base sentence: The cat shivered on the corner. This activity also integrates children’s vocabulary building. (low) 
Fix-It Fast – Show or say a sentence with a missing part or jumbled order. Students help you quickly fix it together using previously learned sentence structures under review. (middle)
The Missing Structure? – Say or show a mix of sentences missing structures children previously learned (e.g., subjects, verbs, adjectives, prepositional phrases, etc). Students give a thumbs up if it’s there, and thumbs down if it is not.  Optional: Have children add in the structure if it’s missing. (middle)
Build and Say – Give students word cards or visuals (subject, verb, object) and have them build a sentence and say it out loud. Keep it simple and quick. (middle)
Silly Sentence Swap – Read a sentence and swap one word to make it silly. Students tell you what part was changed (subject, verb, etc.). (middle)
Act It Out Quick – Read a sentence and have students act it out quickly, then review who did what to reinforce sentence parts. (middle)
Stretch and Repeat – Say a short sentence, and ask students to stretch it by adding one of the sentence structure parts they learned (e.g., an adjective or compound verb). Repeat a few times. (high)
Sentence Snap – Flash a sentence for a few seconds. Remove it, then ask students what the sentence was about or to identify a key sentence structure part, like the verb or delayed adjective. (high)
Paint Your Sentence – Children choose one of their vocabulary words, identify its part of speech, and correctly create and write a simple sentence using that word. The teacher then tells children they have a one minute challenge to add one more structure to their sentence (e.g., compound verb, prepositional phrase, adverb clause). (High–Kindergarten level or First Grade) 

Guided Practice Activities


#Use Guided Practice Activities to respond to teacher prompting for modification of activity according to child ability level. 

Same or Different: Teacher displays different types of sentences using previously learned sentence structures along with the targeted sentence structure. Children must listen to the sentence and identify whether the sentence includes the targeted structure (Same) or if  it doesn’t include the targeted structure (Different). (low)  
Sentence Hunt: Children are shown or read several sentences and must find the ones that match a specific sentence structure (e.g., subject + action verb). (low)
Build-A-Sentence: Children are guided to create their own sentence by imitating a model (e.g., The whale is  a ___). The teacher provides scaffolds such as visuals, word banks, or sentence frames to help children generate original content using a targeted sentence structure. (middle) 
Sentence Scrambler: Children receive individual words or sentence parts (on cards or digital pieces) and must arrange them to form a complete sentence that matches a target structure. (middle) 
Stretchy Sentence: Children start with a short base sentence (e.g., The dolphin dives) and are prompted to “stretch” it by adding details using a specific structure (e.g., adding an adjective, prepositional phrase, or conjunction). The teacher may ask guiding questions like “Where does the dolphin dive?” to support elaboration. (middle)
Magic Sentence Wand: Children are given two short, related sentences (e.g., Manatees eat seagrass. Manatees eat algae.) and use a “magic wand” (or verbal prompt) to combine them into one sentence using a target structure (e.g., Manatees eat seagrass and algae.). (high) 
Echo Sentence: The teacher reads or presents a model sentence, and children generate their own sentence that follows the same pattern but changes the content (e.g., The manatee is a mammal. → The sealion is a predator.). Children may say or write their version. (high) 
Paint Your Sentence – Children choose one of their vocabulary words, identify its part of speech, and correctly create and write a simple sentence using that word. The teacher then tells children they have a one minute challenge to add one more structure to their sentence (e.g., compound verb, prepositional phrase, adverb clause). (High–Kindergarten level or First Grade) 
Two Sentence Build-It Challenge: Children build a sentence with a targeted sentence  structure. Then they must create another sentence that builds on the first sentence and also incorporates a previously learned sentence structure. (high)

## FORM M: Elements of the Genre

THIS IS FORM M.
Elements of Genre 

	-	Informational/Explanatory Text 
1. Informational / Explanatory Text  Children write to share real information about a topic they know or are learning about. This type of writing helps them explain facts, ideas, or how something works.  Example: Writing about animals that live in the ocean or how pumpkins grow or the life cycle of a butterfly.
Elements of Genre to teach to support idea generation and organization
Writing a topic sentence (Main Idea) = Topic + What is the main thing you want to explain about the topic (The bodies of polar bears help them live in the Artic)
Relevant & Irrelevant Details = details that explain and elaborate the topic. (Polar bears have thick fur to keep them warm vs. Penguins are good swimmers) 
Fact vs. Fantasy = facts that are real and observable not make believe. (Polar bears have thick fur to keep them warm vs. Polar bears go to school and play with their friends at recess).
Structure = Main Idea + supporting details + Conclusion 
2. Reading Response (Summary / Recall) Children respond to a book they read or heard by telling what it was about. This may include retelling key events. Example: After reading The Very Hungry Caterpillar, they write or draw to show what happened first, next, and last.
Elements of Genre to teach to support idea generation and organization
Characters & Setting = identifying who the characters are and where are they 
Textual Evidence = getting kids to understand that they are summarizing what the text says not what they make up on their own. 
Sequence of events = identifying chronological order, what happened 1st, 2nd, and 3rd, etc. 
Problem & Resolution = what was the main problem/conflict of the story and how did the problem get solved.
Structure = Beginning, Middle, and End = identify main details of a story in order. 
3. Compare and Contrast  Children look at two things and describe how they are the same and how they are different. This builds thinking and language skills.  Example: Writing about how mammals and fish are alike and different, or comparing two characters from a story.
Elements of Genre to teach to support idea generation and organization
Writing a Main Idea topic sentence = Topic + What is the main thing you want to explain about the topic (Polar bears and Black bears are different types of bears)
Relevant & Irrelevant Details = details that explain and elaborate the topic. (Polar bears have thick fur to keep them warm vs. Penguins are good swimmers) 
Same supporting details vs. Different supporting details 
Fact vs. Fantasy = facts that are real and observable not make believe. (Polar bears have thick fur to keep them warm vs. Polar bears go to school and play with their friends at recess).
Structure =Topic Sentence (Main idea), supporting details about similarities and differences, conclusion  
	-	Opinion/Argumentative Writing    
1. Personal Opinion Writing  Children share what they like or think and give a reason why. This type of writing encourages them to express preferences or beliefs.  Example: “Recess is my favorite part of school because I can play with my friends. Friends make the day fun. 
Elements of Genre to teach to support idea generation and organization
Writing a topic sentence (Thesis) = Topic + What is the main thing you believe or think about that topic (Polar bears are amazing animals.)
Opinion vs Fact = understanding the difference between a statement that expresses an opinion or belief or conclusion about a topic and a statement that explains a fact. (My favorite mammal is the manatee vs. Manatees float to the surface of the water in order to breathe.)
Relevant & Irrelevant Details = details that explain and elaborate the topic. (Polar bears have thick fur to keep them warm vs. Penguins are good swimmers) 
Structure = Thesis Statement/Opinion Statement  + supporting details (Why?) + Conclusion (the importance of the why to the writer) 
2. Problem–Solution Writing (Cause and Effect Thinking) This is an early version of problem-solution writing. Children describe a problem and imagine a helpful solution. Exploring a problem helps them think through events and how one thing leads to another.  Example: “Littering can cause animals to get hurt. When people throw trash on the ground, animals might think it’s food or get stuck in it. That can make them sick or hurt. We should throw out our trash.”
Elements of Genre to teach to support idea generation and organization
Writing a statement of the problem (Thesis) = Topic + What is the main thing you believe or think about that topic (Ice melting in the arctic can cause polar bears to go extinct).
Opinion vs Fact = understanding the difference between a statement that expresses an opinion or belief or conclusion about a topic and a statement that explains a fact.
Relevant & Irrelevant Details = details that explain and elaborate the topic. (Polar bears have thick fur to keep them warm vs. Penguins are good swimmers) 
Structure = Statement of Problem + Effect of Problem (how does the problem lead to something not good) + Conclusion: Solution to the Problem 

4. Reading Response (Inferring / Drawing Conclusions)  Children think beyond what the text says to explain why something happened or what a character might be thinking or feeling. They use clues from the story to support their ideas.  Example: “I think the boy was sad because he lost his toy. In the text it says, he put his head down, and he’s not smiling.” No one likes to lose a toy.” 
Elements of Genre to teach to support idea generation and organization
Writing a thesis statement for reading response = Topic (answer to the question, e.g., Why is the boy sad = The boy is sad because ..) + What is the main thing you want to explain about the topic (e.g., because he lost his toy).
Cause & Effect Opinion: Why –what would be the effects of a textual event / what are the causes of a textual event.
Relevant & Irrelevant Details = details that explain and elaborate the topic. (Polar bears have thick fur to keep them warm vs. Penguins are good swimmers) 
Textual Evidence = What the text directly says 
Inference = what is the meaning of the text, considering what the text says AND what we know about life in general.
Reading Response Opinion Structure = Answer the Question in a complete statement, textual evidence, Conclusion (connect to what we know in general).

	-	Narrative
1. Personal Narrative  Children write or tell a true story about something that happened to them. It often includes where they were, what they did, and how they felt. This helps build a sense of time, sequence, and self-expression.  Example: “I went to the park. I saw a big dog. I felt scared, but it was nice. I didn’t need to be scared”
Elements of Genre to teach to support idea generation and organization
Writing an exposition = Introduce Character Setting and Problem
Cause and Effect = Rising Action (e.g., how does the main character (the writer) react to the problem?)
Sequence of Events = identifying chronological order, what happened 1st, 2nd, and 3rd, etc.
Conclusion = What did the writer come to understand 
2. Story Writing (Fictional Narrative)
Children make up a story with characters and events. These stories can be realistic or imaginary. Story writing helps develop creativity, language, and understanding of story structure. Example: “Once there was a dragon who loved cupcakes. One day, he ran out, so he flew to the bakery!”
Elements of Genre to teach to support idea generation and organization
Writing an exposition = Introduce Character Setting and Problem
Cause and Effect = Rising Action (e.g., how does the main character react to the problem?)
Sequence of Events = identifying chronological order, what happened 1st, 2nd, and 3rd, etc.
Dialogue = what characters would say to move the plot forward  
Conclusion = How does the problem get resolved 
Descriptive Writing = Sensory details to create tone for readers to feel a mood.

## FORM R: Guide for Sentence Structure in Early Childhood


FORM R 
This is Form R

Guide for Sentence Structure in Early Childhood 

What is Syntax? 
Syntax is how we put words together to make sentences that are clear, complete, and meaningful. We start with short, simple sentences and gradually learn to add more detail and complexity. 
 
Part 1: Foundations – Building Basic Sentences 
Simple Sentences – Subject + action verb (Independent Clause) 
Teach: A complete sentence needs a noun (who/what) and a verb (what they do). 
Example: The dog runs. The dog bolts.  
Start with oral sentences before moving to writing.
Include vocabulary words that are action verbs 
 
Part 2: Describing Nouns with Adjectives 
Add Adjectives 
Teach: Adjectives describe nouns. 
Questions: What kind? How many? 
Examples: 
- The big dog barked. The enormous dog barked (use tier 2 vocabulary words as synonyms for tier 1 words)
- She saw three birds. 
Use real objects or pictures to support concept development. 
 
Part 3: Describing Verbs with One-Word Adverbs 
Add One-Word Adverbs 
Teach: Adverbs describe verbs (actions). 
Questions: 
- How? → quickly, slowly, loudly 
- When? → yesterday, today, soon 
- Where? → outside, here, upstairs 
Examples: 
- He ran quickly. 
- They arrived yesterday. 
- The children played outside. 
Limit to one-word adverbs only at this stage. Use visuals and movement. 
 
Part 4: Using Prepositional Phrases 
Prepositional Phrases as Adverbs 
Teach: Prepositional phrases that describe where or when an action happens. 
Examples: 
- The kids ran in the park. 
- We ate lunch after school. 
Focus on where something happened first –then move to when something happened. 

Common prepositions for where something happened. 

in – The seed is in the soil. 
on – The plant sits on the windowsill. 
under – The roots grow under the ground. 
above – The sun is above the plant. 
next to – The flower is next to the rock. 
behind – The pot is behind the chair. 
in front of – The plant is in front of the fence. 
between – The sprout grows between two stones. 
near – The tree is near the playground. 
inside – The worm is inside the soil. 
outside – We planted seeds outside.

Prepositions that show time (when)
	-	before – Wash your hands before lunch. 
	-	after – We read a book after recess. 
	-	during – We stay quiet during circle time. 
	-	at – We line up at the bell. 
	-	on – We go to PE on Tuesday. 
	-	in – Flowers bloom in spring.
 
Prepositional Phrases as Adjectives 
Teach: Prepositional phrases that describe which one? or what kind? (modifying nouns). 
Example: The girl with the red hat smiled. 

All prepositional phrases that act as adjectives come right after the noun they describe.

Common Prepositions that act as adjectives:

with
of 
by
from 
 
Part 5: Be-Verbs and Sentence Meaning 
 Be-Verbs + Adjective 
Teach: Be-verbs (is, am, are, was, were) link the subject to a description. 
Example: The girl is nice. 
 
Be-Verbs + Noun (Predicate Nominative) 
Teach: A be-verb links the subject to another noun that renames it. 
Example: The manatee is a mammal. 
 
Be-Verbs + Prepositional Phrase (as Adverbial Phrase) 
Teach: A be-verb links the subject to a place or time description. 
Example: The boat is in the water. 
(Note: Avoid progressive forms with action verbs at this stage like "is floating.") 
 
Part 6: Expanding the Sentence Meaning with Action 
Add Direct Objects 
Teach: A direct object receives the action. 
Example: She kicked the ball. 
 
 
Part 7 Opening Adverbs (One Word) 
Teach: Begin a sentence with a one-word adverb to describe how, when, or where something happens. 
Examples: 
- Slowly, she opened the door. 
- Yesterday, we played soccer. 
 
Part 8: Opening Phrase with Multiple Adjectives or Adverbs 
Teach: Begin with a short list of adjectives or adverbs to describe the subject or the action more fully. 
Examples: 
- Happy, proud, and ready, the team took the field. 
- Quickly and quietly, the children lined up. 
 
Opening Phrase with Adjective + Prepositional Phrase 
Teach: Begin with an adjective followed by a prepositional phrase to give more information about the subject. 
Examples: 
- Bright with color, the painting stood out. 
- Ready for lunch, the students found their seats. 
 
Opening Phrase with Adverb + Prepositional Phrase 
Teach: Begin with an adverb followed by a prepositional phrase to describe how or where something happens. 
Examples: 
- Carefully across the bridge, the hikers walked. 
- Loudly in the hallway, the bell rang. 
 
Delayed Detail Phrases 
Delayed Adjective Phrases 
Teach: Adjectives that come after the noun in a list to show feeling or condition. 
Example: The puppy sat, cold, scared, and lonely. 
 
Delayed Adverb Phrases 
Teach: Multiple adverbs placed after the verb for effect. 
Example: She moved, slowly and cautiously. 
 
Delayed Phrases with Adjective + Prepositional Phrase 
Examples: 
- The dog barked, nervous in the dark. 
- The girl smiled, happy with her drawing. 
 
Delayed Phrases with Adverb + Prepositional Phrase 
Teach: Use an adverb followed by a prepositional phrase to describe how and where something happens. 
Examples: 
- She danced across the room, gracefully with light feet. 
- He moved through the hallway, silently without a word. 
 
Part 9: Compound Structures 
Compound Nouns 
Example: The cat and dog ran. 
 
Compound Verbs 
Example: The bird flew and sang. 
 
Compound Direct Objects 
Example: She found a pencil and a notebook. 
 
Part 10: Compound Sentences  
Teach: Combine two independent clauses with one of the FANBOYS (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so). 
Example: He was tired, so he took a nap. 
 
Complex Sentence Features 
Present Participles 
Teach: Descriptive phrases starting with a word ending in -ing. 
Examples: 
- Walking slowly, the boy looked around. 
- Feeling faint, she sat down. 
 
Adverb Clauses 
Teach: Groups of words that explain why, when, where, or how, starting with subordinating conjunctions. 
Example: He cried because he was scared. 
 
Part 11: Abstract and Complex Ideas 
Abstract Nouns 
Teach: Nouns that name feelings or ideas. 
Examples: freedom, hope, honesty 
Example: She showed kindness to the new student. 
 
Noun Clauses 
Teach: Groups of words that act like a noun. 
Examples: 
- What he said made us laugh. 
- I remember that she was kind.
