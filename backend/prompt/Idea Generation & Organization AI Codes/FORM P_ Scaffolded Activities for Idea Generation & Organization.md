**Suggested Activities For Idea Generation & Organization  **

**Review & Connect Activities **

*All of these activities would be appropriate for all levels. The content of discussions and complexity of the skill will increase as children advance in their writing skills. *
**Concept Picture Walk: **Children revisit key images from a shared text, anchor chart, or concept map. The teacher guides students to recall facts using questions like “What do you see?” or “What do you remember?” Children generate ideas by connecting pictures to unit concepts. This supports idea generation by activating background knowledge through visuals and guided discussion.
**Video Clip + Think-Pair-Share: **Children view a short video clip related to the unit topic and connected to texts being read (e.g., penguins in the cold). The teacher pauses to ask targeted questions that prompt children to think and talk with a partner. This helps them recall facts, build vocabulary, and connect new information to class learning.
**Genre Song or Chant (to the tune of “If You’re Happy and You Know It”)
** Children sing a repetitive, upbeat chant set to the familiar melody of *"If You're Happy and You Know It."* For example, for informational writing, the chant could be:
🎵
 “If it’s real and it’s true, it’s a *fact* (clap, clap)
 If it teaches something new, it’s a *fact* (clap, clap)
 If you’re writing to explain,
 And your facts are super plain,
 Then you’re working in the *information* lane!” (clap, clap)
 🎵
The teacher pairs the chant with visuals (e.g., a picture of a nonfiction book, animals, or diagrams) and gestures (e.g., pointing to the brain for "think," clapping for rhythm, miming writing or reading). This multisensory approach helps children internalize the purpose and structure of the genre through rhythm, movement, and repetition.
**Home-to-School Sharing Circle: **Children connect personal or family experiences to the topic of study (e.g., sharing what they wear in winter). The teacher helps children link these experiences to the genre and unit content (e.g., “Just like you wear coats, penguins have feathers to stay warm.”). This builds relevance and supports idea development.
**Genre Poster Sort: **Children help sort sentence strips or images into categories (e.g., informational or narrative) based on what they learned about the features of each genre. This supports genre recognition and reinforces the kind of writing children will produce.

**Guided Practice Activities**

*Guided practice has children engage in a cognitive process and use a particular skill or skills to complete a task in sequential chunks or steps that is monitored by the teacher. The teacher can provide feedback and the child can practice with teacher support. The teacher gradually intensifies what the child is doing by releasing control so that the child is doing more on their own until they can apply independently. A modification of any of these activities could be breaking down steps in smaller components for children who need more support or increasing the complexity of the task so that children are engaging with multiple components of the task at once for children who are advanced. Another modification is to **alter the instruction. Children who need more practice practice will need to do more examples with the teacher to get to the point of application. *

**Sentence Stem Topic Sentence:** Children are given a sentence starter (e.g., “Penguins can survive the cold because...”) and guided to complete it using information from shared readings or discussions. The teacher provides visual aids, oral rehearsal, and modeled responses to help children form complete topic sentences. (low)
**Two Topic Sentences – Match the Details Level 1:
** **Level 1:** Children are shown two different topic sentences with clearly distinct subjects (e.g., “Penguins’ bodies help them survive in their habitat” and “Tigers live in hot places like the jungle”) along with three or four supporting details. With teacher guidance, children match the details to the correct topic sentence using images, shared reading content, or sorting cards. (low)

**Two Topic Sentences – Match the Details Level 2:**
 **Level 2:** Children are shown two topic sentences about the same subject but with different purposes (e.g., “Penguins’ bodies help them survive in the cold” and “Penguins live in groups to stay safe”) and are given three or four related supporting details. With teacher support, they determine which detail best fits each topic sentence by reasoning through the specific focus of each sentence. (middle)

**Two Topic Sentences – Match the Details  Level 3:
** **Level 3:** Children are shown two closely related topic sentences (e.g., “Penguins use teamwork to survive” and “Penguins are built for life in the ocean”) and are given four or more supporting details that require thoughtful sorting. With teacher prompting, children justify their matches using because-statements and generate an additional sentence that fits one of the topic sentences. (high)
**Two Topic Sentences – Give Notes – Choose Best Topic Sentence Level 1:** Children are given a set of notes that contain facts/details or pictures representing the facts/details. Then, the teacher presents two topic sentences, and children decide which one best fits the details. (low) 
**Two Topic Sentences – Give Notes – Choose Best Topic Sentence Level 2:** Children are given a set of facts or pictures. They help take notes using drawings or short phrases (e.g., “feathers = warm,” “eat fish”). Then, the teacher presents two topic sentences, and children decide which one best fits their notes. Lastly, they generate their own topic sentence using a sentence stem. (middle-high)
**One Topic – Details – Which One Doesn’t Belong?
 Level 1: **Children are shown a set of images or facts with one very obvious off-topic detail (e.g., topic: “How penguins survive” — details include: “Penguins huddle to stay warm,” “Penguins have thick feathers,” “Tigers hunt in the jungle”). With teacher support, children identify the detail that doesn’t belong and explain their thinking. (low)
**One Topic – Details – Which One Doesn’t Belong?
 Level 2: **Children are given a set of facts or images where the irrelevant detail is subtly off-topic (e.g., topic: “How penguins survive” — details include: “Penguins slide on their bellies,” “Penguins eat fish,” “Penguins have black and white feathers,” “Penguins play with snowballs”). With teacher prompting, children identify and remove the detail that doesn’t fit and explain how it is less related to survival. (middle)
**One Topic – Details – Which One Doesn’t Belong?
 Level 3: **Children are given a set of four to six written or illustrated details on a topic with one or two that are slightly off-purpose or loosely connected (e.g., topic: “How penguins survive” — details include: “Penguins have thick feathers,” “They live in the Antarctic,” “Penguins are birds,” “Penguins huddle in groups”). Children identify which detail(s) do not support the topic’s main purpose (survival), justify their choice with reasoning, and suggest a stronger replacement detail. (high)
**Note-Taking of Relevant Textual Details (by Genre):** Children revisit a shared text and write simple words to and draw representing pictures to take notes on key genre-specific details:
**Facts** for informational text
**Supporting details **for opinion and response to reading texts
**Problem and cause/effect** of the problem for problem-solution texts
**Steps or events** for sequencing texts
**Story events** for narrative texts
 The teacher provides visual support and models how to convert what they hear or see into simple notes. (low-middle)

**Two Topic Sentences – Give Notes – Choose Best Topic Sentence Level 2:** Children are given a set of facts or pictures. They help take notes using drawings or short phrases (e.g., “feathers = warm,” “eat fish”). Then, the teacher presents two topic sentences, and children decide which one best fits their notes. Lastly, they generate their own topic sentence using a sentence stem. (high)
**Categorize Notes – Point 1 and Point 2:** Children categorize facts or pictures under two points or big ideas (e.g., Point 1: How penguins stay warm; Point 2: How penguins find food). The teacher models and supports categorizing using anchor charts and sorting mats. (middle-high)
**Relevant vs. Irrelevant Match (Level 1):
 **Children are given a clear topic sentence (e.g., “Penguins live in cold places”) and four to six details. Some are clearly off-topic (e.g., “Tigers live in the jungle”), and others are directly related (e.g., “Penguins slide on their bellies over ice”). With teacher guidance and visual supports, children sort the details into relevant and irrelevant categories to reinforce paragraph unity using distinct and unrelated content. (low)
**Relevant vs. Irrelevant Match (Level 2):
 **Children are given a topic sentence (e.g., “Penguins’ bodies help them survive the cold”) and a set of closely related but varied supporting details. Some details are relevant but others, while still about penguins, don’t directly support the main idea (e.g., “Penguins eat krill” vs. “Penguins have waterproof feathers to keep them dry and warm”). Children work with the teacher to evaluate how well each detail supports the central idea, deepening their understanding of focused writing. (middle)
**Topic Sentence – Relevant vs. Irrelevant Match Level 3:** Children are shown a topic sentence and a set of related supporting details. All details are generally on-topic, but vary in how directly they support the central idea. With teacher guidance, children evaluate which details are the strongest support, eliminate loosely connected ones, and revise weak or off-topic details to improve paragraph focus and clarity.
 Example: Topic Sentence: “Penguins have body parts that help them survive in cold habitats.”
 Details to Evaluate:** **Penguins have thick feathers that trap heat. (correct) Penguins waddle when they walk. (not really–Revise: “Waddling helps penguins move across ice.”) Penguins eat krill and fish. (incorrect) (Related to diet, not body parts) A layer of blubber keeps penguins warm. (correct)
 Teacher Prompt: “Which details are the strongest support? Can we fix the ones that are off-topic?”
 (high)
**Which Sentence Builds the Topic? (Level 1):
** Children are shown a simple topic sentence (e.g., “Polar bears are strong animals”) and two possible next sentences: one that clearly connects (e.g., “They use their big paws to walk on ice”) and one that is clearly off-topic (e.g., “Some birds migrate to warm places”). With support, they choose the sentence that logically builds on the topic, practicing simple paragraph structure. (low)
**Which Sentence Builds the Topic? (Level 2):
 **Children are given a topic sentence (e.g., “Arctic foxes survive by blending into their surroundings”) and two closely related next-sentence options: one that logically builds the idea (e.g., “Their white fur hides them in the snow from predators”) and one that is loosely related but less focused (e.g., “They eat small animals like lemmings”). Children discuss which detail best supports the topic and why, encouraging precision in idea development. (middle)
**Which Sentence Builds the Topic? (Level 3): **Children are shown a topic sentence and three possible next sentences that vary in how directly they build on the idea. With teacher support, they are guided to evaluate the options, select the sentence that most clearly supports or expands on the topic sentence, and justify their reasoning (e.g., “This one connects because it tells how the hare hides”). As an extension, children may revise a weak sentence or generate their own improved next sentence to support deeper understanding of paragraph development. (**Example: **Topic Sentence: “The Arctic hare uses camouflage to avoid predators.” Next Sentence Options: A. “Its white fur blends in with the snow.” (correct) B. “Hares hop quickly across the snow.” (Not really- Not directly about camouflage) C. “The Arctic is a very cold place.”  (Incorrect–Not focused on the hare or camouflage). Teacher Prompt: “Which one builds on the idea of camouflage? Can you explain why? Can you write a better next sentence?” (high)
**Circle the Cohesion – Words That Connect ****Level 1: Early Awareness of Connecting Words
** Children are presented with a short, teacher-written sentence pair or very simple paragraph using basic cohesive words (e.g., *and, but, then*). The teacher reads the text aloud with clear expression and models how these words help ideas stick together. Using a shared chart or sentence strip, the teacher prompts children to **circle or highlight** each cohesive word together as a group. As each word is identified, the teacher explains its function using visuals or gestures (e.g., “*Then* tells us what happened next”).
 **Example:
** Text: “The penguin slid on the ice, **and** it splashed into the water.”
 **Prompt:** “Let’s circle the word that sticks these two actions together. What does *and* help us know?”
 (low)
**Circle the Cohesion – Words That Connect ****Level 2: Identify and Compare Types of Cohesion
** Children work with a short modeled paragraph (3–4 sentences) that includes **different types of cohesive devices**, such as **addition (also), contrast (but), cause (because),** and **time (then, after)**. After the teacher reads the text aloud, children work with a partner or as a class to **circle each cohesive word** and **discuss its role** in connecting the ideas. The teacher prompts children to explain how these words help the sentences make sense as a whole.
 **Example:
** Text: “Penguins huddle together to stay warm. **Also**, they have thick feathers. They can still get cold **because** the wind is strong.”
 **Prompt:** “Which word shows us a new idea that goes with the first? Which word tells us why they get cold?”
 (middle-high)
**Circle the Cohesion – Words That Connect ****Level 3: Evaluate and Revise for Cohesion
** Children are given a teacher-written paragraph that includes **some strong cohesive devices** and **some weak or missing connections**. After reading the paragraph aloud, students work in pairs or small groups to **identify and underline existing cohesive devices**, **discuss their function**, and then **revise the paragraph by adding or improving cohesive words**. They justify their choices by explaining how the added words help the ideas flow.
 **Example:
** Original Text: “Penguins dive into the water. They catch fish. The water is cold. They swim fast.”
 Student Revision: “Penguins dive into the water. **Then**, they catch fish. **Although** the water is cold, they swim fast.”
 **Prompt:** “Where could we add a word to help the ideas connect more smoothly? Why does that word help?”
 (high)
**Build the Flow – Rearranging for Cohesion ****Level 1: One Idea, Right Order
**Children are presented with a pair of related sentences or sentence parts that are jumbled. The teacher reads the parts aloud and models thinking: “Which sentence comes first? Which one adds on?” Using visuals or movement (e.g., sentence strips on a pocket chart), children rearrange the sentence parts so the second idea clearly builds upon the first. The teacher emphasizes how one sentence leads to the next using cohesive logic (e.g., time, cause, or contrast).
** **Example:
 Jumbled: “He is cold. The penguin jumps into the icy water.”
 Corrected: “The penguin jumps into the icy water. He is cold.”
 Prompt: “Which one tells what happened first? Which one explains the result?” (low)
**Build the Flow – Rearranging for Cohesion ****Level 2: Short Paragraph Sequencing with Logical Connections
** Children are given 3–4 mixed-up sentences about a familiar topic. Working with a partner or in a small group, they read the sentences aloud, then rearrange them in the order that makes the most sense. The teacher prompts students to look for clues like time words (first, then), cause/effect (because, so), or comparisons (but, also) to guide sequencing. Children read their revised paragraph aloud and explain how one idea builds on the next.
 Example (jumbled): **The penguin dives deep under the water. It sees a shiny fish swimming by. It is hungry. It catches the fish in its beak.
**Correct Order: “The penguin is hungry. It dives deep under the water. It sees a shiny fish swimming by. It catches the fish in its beak.”
 Prompt: “Which sentence gives us the reason? What happened next? What sentence brings it to a close?**” **(middle)
**Build the Flow – Rearranging for Cohesion ****Level 3: Fill the Gaps – Writing Cohesive Links
**Children are given a paragraph with key cohesive elements missing. Some sentences are in the correct order, but the logical flow between them is unclear. Students are tasked with adding their own connecting phrases or transitional words (e.g., *First, Then, Because of this, Although*) in the blank spaces to improve cohesion. They can also revise sentence structure for clarity and better flow. Afterward, they read their version aloud and explain how their additions help the ideas connect.
 Example:
 Text with blanks:
 “The penguin is cold. ___________, it dives into the water to catch fish. ___________, it uses its wings to swim fast. It eats the fish quickly. ___________, it pops back up onto the ice.”
 Student Fill-in Example:
 “The penguin is cold. Still, it dives into the water to catch fish. Then, it uses its wings to swim fast. It eats the fish quickly. After that, it pops back up onto the ice.”
 Prompt: “What words can we add so the story feels smoother? How do your words help us understand the penguin’s actions?” (high)
**Fact vs. Fantasy – Distinguishing Real from Make-Believe**** Level 1: Two Choices – What’s Real?
**At this beginning level, the teacher introduces the concept of real versus pretend information using vivid examples and concrete language. Children are shown two choices at a time—either through images, sentence cards, or oral storytelling. One option reflects a factual detail about a real-world animal, place, or process, and the other represents an imaginative or fantasy element (e.g., talking animals, magical events). The teacher reads both aloud, then prompts:
 “Which one could really happen in the world? Which one is just for fun or make-believe?” Children point, circle, or sort the correct choice with guidance.Example: “A penguin swims underwater to catch fish.” ✅
“A penguin uses a magic wand to fly in the sky.” ❌ The teacher explains why the first detail is based on real-life animal behavior, while the second is make-believe. This activity may be done in small group or circle time with picture support. (low)
**Fact vs. Fantasy – Distinguishing Real from Make-Believe**** Level 2: Sort the Details – Fact or Fantasy?
 **Children are given a mixed set of 6–8 sentence or image cards related to a specific topic (e.g., “How animals survive in the cold” or “What happens in the ocean”). Some statements are grounded in real-world facts, while others include fantasy elements. In groups or pairs, students sort the cards into two categories: *Fact* and *Fantasy*. The teacher then checks with students and asks questions like: “How do you know that’s real?” or “What makes this one sound pretend?” Example Card Set: “A polar bear builds a den in the snow.” ✅“A snowflake talks to a baby seal.” ❌ “Walruses rest together on ice.” ✅ “Penguins wear winter hats and scarves.” ❌(middle)
**Fact vs. Fantasy – Distinguishing Real from Make-Believe**** Level 3: Revise the Fantasy – Make It Real
 **At this level, children work with sentences that contain imaginative or fantasy details, and their task is to revise or rewrite the sentences to make them factually accurate. The teacher first models one or two examples, showing how to replace silly or magical parts with real-world information. Then children are given 3–5 sentences containing imaginative elements and prompted to “make them real” using what they know about the topic. Example: Original: “The penguin danced with a snowman at the North Pole.” Revised: “The penguin slid on the ice to find its food.” Children are encouraged to use content knowledge, shared texts, or classroom anchor charts to support revision. The teacher may prompt: “What part is make-believe? What could happen instead?” (high)
**Level 1: Two Choices – What’s a Fact? What’s an Opinion?
** At this introductory level, the teacher presents children with two simple sentences at a time—one factual and one opinion-based—paired with visuals and read aloud in a shared format. The teacher models the difference using sentence stems such as “A fact is something that is true and can be proven” and “An opinion is how someone feels or what they like.” Children are prompted to choose which sentence is the fact and which is the opinion, often by pointing, circling, or sorting with teacher support. For example: “Penguins live in cold places” (fact) vs. “Penguins are the cutest animals” (opinion). This level focuses on recognition with guided discussion and clear cues to help children distinguish between what is true for everyone and what is a personal feeling. (low)
**Level 2: Sort Multiple Sentences – Fact or Opinion?
** At this level, children are given a set of 6–8 short sentences and work independently or in small groups to sort them into “Fact” and “Opinion” categories using a pocket chart, sentence strips, or individual paper sort. Sentences are topic-linked (e.g., animals, weather, or food) and include both straightforward facts and clear opinions. Students may be provided with visual anchors or sentence stems such as “I think…” or “It is true that…” to help guide their decision-making. The teacher prompts students to explain how they know a sentence is a fact (e.g., “because we read it in a book” or “because it can be proven”) versus an opinion (“because people can feel differently about it”). Example sentences might include: “Walruses have tusks” (fact) vs. “Walruses look funny” (opinion). This level encourages reasoning and comparison while reinforcing academic vocabulary. (middle)
**Level 3: Create or Revise – Make a Fact or Make an Opinion
** At the most advanced level, children are given mixed sentences that are vague or imprecise and asked to revise them into clearer facts or stronger opinions, or to generate their own original examples of each. The teacher first models how to strengthen a sentence like “Penguins are nice” into “In my opinion, penguins are interesting because they waddle and swim.” Then children work independently or in pairs to rewrite 3–5 mixed sentences. Alternatively, they may be given a neutral sentence and asked to turn it into both a fact and an opinion version. For example: Given “Penguins live in Antarctica,” a child might write, “Fact: Penguins live in the Antarctic, where it is cold,” and “Opinion: I think penguins are the best animals in the snow.” This level emphasizes critical thinking, sentence-level writing, and clear use of evidence or personal perspective.
 (high)

**Sequencing – Logical Order of Events:** Children receive pictures or sentence strips representing steps in a process (e.g., “How penguins hatch”) or a story sequence. They help arrange the items in the correct order, using ordinal words like *first*, *then*, and *last*.
**Dialogue Match:** Children match lines of dialogue to the correct character or situation (e.g., “I’m cold!” goes with a penguin chick picture). The teacher discusses how dialogue helps show what characters feel or do.
**Dialogue Rating Scale (OK, Good, Wow):** The teacher presents three levels of dialogue examples. Children listen and vote or sort them as “OK,” “Good,” or “Wow!” based on how much the dialogue reveals about the character or story. This builds awareness of expressive and purposeful dialogue.
**Descriptive Grammar Find:** Children are shown an image and asked to find or listen for describing words used by the teacher. For example, the teacher describes a penguin scene, and children point out or list words like *fluffy*, *cold*, *slippery*. Word banks or anchor charts help scaffold vocabulary.
**Descriptive Grammar Puzzle:** Children are given word or phrase cards (e.g., *cold feathers*, *slippery ice*, *waddling penguin*) and help build descriptive sentences by combining the pieces. The teacher models how these combinations create strong mental images.
**Does This Ending Make Sense?:** Children are read a story beginning and then two different endings. They decide which ending makes sense and explain why. This helps build understanding of story structure and logical closure.
**What Conflict Can Come From This Setting?:** Children are shown an image of a setting (e.g., icy land, stormy sea) and brainstorm with the teacher what problems could happen in that place. This supports early narrative planning.
**What Conflict Can Come From This Character?:** Children are shown a character (e.g., a scared penguin chick) and help predict what kind of trouble or challenge that character might face. This sparks imagination and story idea generation.
**Cause and Effect Narrative Conflict:** Children explore story problems through simple cause-and-effect models (e.g., “It was very cold → The penguin got lost → He needed to find shelter”). The teacher guides them to build short story chains.
**Build the Suspense – Order the Intensity:** Children are given a set of events with different levels of excitement or danger. They work with the teacher to order the events from least to most suspenseful, learning how to build tension in a story. (high)
