

Rubric for Lesson Plan Generator 

> **Criteria: “Quality” How well the generator can utilize the principles of the science of learning and instruction to provide detailed scripts that can support teacher learning of the what to teach and the how to teach it most effectively.**
>
> Example from Script

---

> **The activities involved should be sequenced in a logical way, starting with easier skills and building in difficulty both within and across activities**
>
> **Within the activity **
> Moving from *Sounds Sparks* (children pronounce the sound) to *Write that Sound* children write the sounds) during the review activity section.
> Pronouncing phonemes is an easier task than recalling a phoneme, its matching grapheme, and writing.
> **Between activities **
> *From Model, Explain, Engage: *
> Let’s do Stretch the Sounds, so we can practice how to listen for /b/ at the beginning or end.
> I am going to do it first and then you repeat.
> My turn: My word is *big*. Stretch the Sounds. (Teacher says each sound /b/ /i/ /g/, while holding up a finger for every sound, then repeat the word-*big*).
> Your turn. Go. (children say each sound /b/ /i/ /g/, while holding up a finger for every sound, then repeat the word-*big*).
> *From Application: *
> Okay. Let's try.
> My turn. My first word is bag. Stretch the sound everyone.
> First sound. (children say the /b/ sound) Write the letter (children write letter b)
> Second sound. (children say the /a/ sound). What’s the letter? (Children say a). Yes. It’s already written for you.
> Third sound. (children say the /g/ sound). Write the letter. (children write the letter g).
> The activity from the Model, Explain and Engage section was priming children brain to segment words int their individual sounds so that children could transfer that process to spelling CVC words in the application section

---

> **The activities should be organized in a way which reflect logical scaffolding: complex processes are broken down into component parts both within and across activities. The activities should reflect gradual release of support from the teacher.**
>
> **Within the activity **
> Let’s do our **Say and Trace Write.** Say the steps as you trace the letter b. Everyone put your pencil at the top line on the left where you see the first b. You want to start here. {Teacher points to top left-hand line of the first b for tracing}
> Let me see. {Teacher corrects students if needed}. Ready. Say and trace. Go. (Children trace the letter as they say the verbal cues “Straight line down. Put on the belly.”) {Teacher monitors children’s letter writing}.
> Now let’s write without tracing. Say the steps as you write the letter b. We are going to do three.
> Start at the left hand side. Put your pencil on the top line where the dot is. The dot at the top line is where you will start.
> Ready. Write and Say. Go.
> (Children write the letter as they say the verbal cues “Straight line down. Put on the belly.”).
> And what is the sound, everyone? (Children say the /b/ sound).
> Children go from tracing, to using a dot at the top of the line, reflecting gradual release of support.
> **Between activities**
> Reviewing letters short a, e, i, o and consonant to be used in words for children to spell in the application section
> Having children isolate beginning and ending sound /b/ to break down the process of later spelling: My word is cab. I am thinking to myself where do I hear the /b/ sound, at the beginning or the end of the word. First, I am going to stretch the sounds in my head: c-a-b. I hear /c/ at the beginning, but I hear /b/ at the end: c-a-b. So, I am going to slide my ball card into the ending box {teacher demonstrates}.
> Increasing the steps in the process to spell:
> Boys and girls, remember *stretch the sound*s. Now I am going to say a word. You are going to stretch those sounds and spell the word. We are doing three sound words. Consonant-vowel-consonant. I am going to remind you of the vowels you know so far {Teacher displays the a, e, i, and o vowels}
> Where are we going to hear these vowels, the beginning, middle, or end of the word? Everyone. Right. These are the vowels you hear in the middle of the word. We are going to do stretch and spell. You are going to stretch the sounds of word. Look at your Stretch-Sound handwriting paper. {Teacher directs students’ attention to the handwriting sheet}. How many sounds will we hear if these are CVC words. Everyone. Yes. three sounds. That’s why we see three boxes for each sound. You first stretch the sounds, then write the letter for each sound.

>
> The following three examples show an increase in difficulty based upon a) the difficulty in skill (e.g., recalling multiple letters, vs. two to three), and b) difficulty of the process (e.g., identifying vs. multi-step spelling)
> Low

We are listening for whether the word begins with a /b/ sound or /p/ sound, but this time we are not going to lift our sound card, we are going to write the letter b for the /b/ sound or the letter p for the /p/ sound. Look at you sound hunt handwriting sheet. You see that in each line there is either a b or a p to trace. I am going to say a word. Then I will stretch the word. You are going to chop that beginning sound to hear whether it is the /b/ sound or the /p/ sound. Then, you are going to trace the correct letter for the beginning sound you hear.

> **The generated lesson plan should include explanations to children why their answer is correct after the first and/or second elicited response from children during an activity**
>
> My turn. My word is bet.
> Let me stretch: /b/-/et/).
> Chop the beginning. (children say /b/)
> Does /b/-et start with the /b/ or /p/ sound? (children should say /b/)
> Yes. I am pushing out air and using my vocal box /b/, so I am going to hold up my ball sound card for the /b/ sound.

---

> **The activities and activity examples should take into account children’s prior practice and learned skills.**
>
> *Do not use sounds that have not been directly taught. Do not use words that have sounds that have not been directly taught.*
> The generator should know what skills children are working on from prompting teacher, and consider prior lessons the teacher has created.

---

> **When appropriate, the activity examples should pair sounds and letters in way that can support children’s comparing and contrasting of phonemes and letter forms for deeper processing**
>
> Remember that we said that the /b/ sound is a lot like the /p/ sound.
> Look at my mouth and listen to the sound of each one.
> Ready. /b/
> Ready. /p/
> What’s different? (Children should respond that one uses their voice)
> Right. For the /b/ sound, I have my lips together and push out air and use my vocal box. For the /p/ sound I still push my lips together and push out air, but I do not use my vocal box.
> Everyone put your hand on your vocal box and make the /b/ sound.
> Everyone.
> Now let’s make the /p/ sound
> Everyone.
> The /b/ sound uses our vocal box. We can feel it when we say /b/.
> Children later on the lesson must distinguish between these two sounds.

> **The activity examples should be chosen to logically support children’s completion of later tasks**
>
> Reviewing sounds and letters that children must recall in later activities

> **The generated lesson should include considerations of where young children may make errors and provide possible corrective feedback.**
>
> Corrective Feedback: Some children may confuse the direction of d, writing instead b or p.
> Teacher: You are close. My sound is /d/. Everyone look up here. Some of you wrote this. {Teacher writes b}. This was our new sound we learned yesterday. This sound is /b/. We are writing /d/. This is /d/ {Teacher writes d on the board}. What is different about these two letters? Everyone think. Partner 1 tell Partner 2. Stop. Partner 2 tell Partner 1. Stop. Jesslyn, what is different about the /b/ and /d/? (child should respond that bottom bubble is different).
> Exactly, both the /d/ and /b/ have a straight line down, but one has a bubble on the back, the left, and one has a bubble in the front, on the right. Everyone say /d/. Where’s the bubble, on the front or back? Everyone (on the back). Everyone say /b/. Where’s the bubble? Everyone. (on the front). *Make sure to bring back d later in the review.

---

> **The generated lesson plan should always provide detailed modeling or a link for example detailed modeling. Detailed modeling is not just demonstrating directions to a task. It is detailing what is happening in the thinking process to complete a task.**
>
> Let me show you. My turn. The word is cab. So, I first stretch the sounds, c-a-b, the beginning sound is /c/. I know that the /c/ sound is written with the letter c. I am going to write c in the first block. So, we have /c/-/a/. The /a/ sound is written with the letter a, so I write the letter a, remember that’s our vowel, in the middle box. So, we have /c/-/a/-/b/. That’s our new sound at the end. I am going to write the letter b for the /b/ sound in the last box. Okay. Let's try.
